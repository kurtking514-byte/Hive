EMERGENCY PREPAREDNESS
★ Emergency preparedness: the steps you take to make sure you are safe before, during, and after an emergency or natural disaster.
★ Natural disasters: floods, landslides, storm surges, and earthquakes.
★ Man-made disasters: explosions, fires, and chemical and biological attacks.
⚙ Disasters can happen anytime, anywhere, and without warning, so plan ahead: supplies, contact information, and escape routes.
⚙ Why prepare: preparation is the best way of coping with devastating long-term effects. After a disaster, help may be days or weeks away.
⚙ How to prepare: assemble a disaster supply kit before disaster strikes, so you can endure an evacuation or home confinement.

UNSAFE ACTS AND UNSAFE CONDITIONS
⚙ Accidents: 98% preventable, 2% non-preventable.
⚙ Causes: 88% unsafe acts, 10% unsafe conditions.
⚙ The numbers add up to 98%, the preventable part.
★ Unsafe act: something a person does that can cause an accident, such as ignoring safety rules.
★ Unsafe condition: something wrong in the surroundings that can cause an accident, such as a wet floor or broken wiring.
Reasons for unsafe acts:
⚙ Improper attitude.
⚙ Lack of knowledge and skill.
⚙ Physical unsuitability.
⚙ Improper mechanical or physical environment.

DIFFERENT HAZARDS
★ Hazard: anything that can cause harm, injury, or damage.
Earthquake hazards:
⚙ Ground shaking, ground rupture, liquefaction, earthquake-induced ground subsidence, tsunami, earthquake-induced landslide.
★ Liquefaction: shaking turns wet, loose soil into something like liquid, so buildings sink or tilt.
★ Ground subsidence: the ground sinks.
★ Ground rupture: the ground surface breaks along a fault.
Fire hazards:
⚙ Fire triangle, causes of fires, phases of a fire emergency.
Added: the fire triangle is heat, fuel, and oxygen. Remove one and the fire goes out.
Workplace hazards:
⚙ Direct (physical): unguarded machines, falling objects, noise, temperature extremes, extreme pressures, vibrations.
⚙ Indirect, chemical: vapors, gas, dust, fumes.
⚙ Indirect, biological: bacteria, virus.
Factors in accident prevention:
⚙ Education about accidents; personal factors; engineering factors; enforcement of safety rules and regulations.

EARTHQUAKE PREPAREDNESS
★ Earthquake preparedness: measures at the individual, organizational, and societal level to minimize the effects of an earthquake, such as securing heavy objects, structural changes, storing supplies, insurance, an emergency kit, and evacuation plans.
Before:
⚙ Purchase earthquake kits and supplies; customize your kit; replace expired supplies; make a preparedness plan; secure hazards.
During, indoors: Duck, Cover, and Hold (DCH).
⚙ Duck under a sturdy desk or table, take cover, and hold on until the shaking stops. If it moves, move with it.
⚙ Stay away from windows, bookcases, file cabinets, heavy mirrors, hanging plants, and other heavy objects.
⚙ No table near you: cover your face and head with your arms and crouch in an inside corner.
⚙ In bed: hold on and protect your head with a pillow, unless a heavy light fixture is above you.
⚙ High-rise building: move against an interior wall and protect your head. Glass windows can dislodge.
⚙ Crowded store: do not rush for exits; move away from shelves.
⚙ Gym or theater: stay in your seat, protect your head, and leave calmly after the shaking.
⚙ Do NOT use elevators. Stay inside until the shaking stops and it is safe.
⚙ Use a doorway only if it is close and you know it is strong and load-bearing.
⚙ Some earthquakes are foreshocks of a larger one.
During, outdoors: stay there.
⚙ Move to a clear area away from trees, signs, buildings, wires, and poles.
⚙ The greatest danger is directly outside buildings, at exits, and along exterior walls.
⚙ On a sidewalk near buildings: duck into a doorway to avoid falling bricks, glass, and debris.
⚙ Driving: pull over and stop away from overpasses, power lines, buildings, and trees; stay in the vehicle.
After:
⚙ Check for injuries and damages; communicate and recover. The days after can be as dangerous as the earthquake.
EASIER: Before, prepare a kit and plan. During, Duck, Cover, and Hold, or get to open space outside. After, check injuries and damage.

FLOODS AND LANDSLIDES
Flood, during:
⚙ Gather emergency supplies and follow radio or TV updates.
⚙ Unplug appliances; turn off gas, power, and water.
⚙ Do NOT drive or walk across flooded roads.
⚙ Tie down or bring outdoor items inside.
⚙ Recognize flood risk: know flood- and landslide-prone areas, warning signals, evacuation routes, and shelters.
Flood, after:
⚙ Throw away items that cannot be disinfected, like wall coverings, cloth, rugs, and drywall.
⚙ Dry with fans, air conditioners, and dehumidifiers.
⚙ Wear rubber boots and plastic gloves for cleanup.
⚙ Clean with soap and water; disinfect with 1 cup of bleach in 1 gallon of water.
⚙ Practice safe hygiene: wash hands with soap and water, and follow officials' advice on safe water.
★ Landslide: masses of rock, earth, or debris moving down a slope.
★ Mudslide (debris flow): a common, fast-moving landslide that flows in channels.
⚙ Landslides come from disturbances in slope stability. They can follow heavy rains, droughts, earthquakes, or volcanic eruptions.
⚙ Slopes where vegetation was destroyed by wildfires or land changes are most vulnerable.

DISASTER MANAGEMENT
★ RA 10121: the Philippine Disaster Risk Reduction and Management Act of 2010, enacted May 27, 2010, to strengthen the Philippine DRR system.
⚙ It covers good governance, risk assessment and early warning, knowledge building and awareness raising, reducing underlying risk factors, and preparedness for effective response and early recovery.
★ Disaster Risk Reduction and Management (DRRM): the systematic process of using administrative directives, organizations, and operational skills and capacities to implement strategies, policies, and improved coping capacities to lessen the adverse impacts of hazards and the possibility of disaster (RA 10121).
⚙ Four phases of emergency management: mitigation, preparedness, response, recovery.
The National DRRM Framework has four areas:
★ Disaster Prevention and Mitigation: avoid hazards and mitigate their impacts by reducing vulnerabilities and exposure and enhancing community capacities.
★ Disaster Preparedness: strengthen communities' capacities to anticipate, cope with, and recover from disasters.
★ Disaster Response: preserve life and meet the basic needs of affected people during or right after a disaster.
★ Disaster Rehabilitation and Recovery: restore and improve facilities, livelihood, and living conditions, following the "build back better" principle.
EASIER: Prevent, prepare, respond, then rebuild better.
Community-based DRRM for preparedness:
⚙ Emergency plan; monitoring and evaluation; early warning systems; survival kits and materials.
Three phases in response to a disaster:
★ Alarm phase: immediate activation of adequate and appropriate resources.
★ Work phase: carrying out the prepared action plan, including rescue (LAST).
★ Let-down phase: recovery of all persons involved, victims and rescuers.
Added: LAST in rescue usually stands for Locate, Access, Stabilize, Transport.

PEOPLE-CENTERED EARLY WARNING SYSTEM
★ Risk knowledge: collect data and do risk assessments. Are hazards and vulnerabilities well known?
★ Monitoring and warning service: monitor hazards and give early warnings. Can accurate and timely warnings be made?
★ Dissemination and communication: communicate risk information and warnings. Do warnings reach everyone at risk, and are they understood?
★ Response capability: build national and community response capabilities. Are people ready to react to warnings?

EMERGENCY RESCUE AND TRANSFER
Indications of emergency rescue (when to move a victim right away):
⚙ Fire or explosion; danger of toxic gases; serious traffic hazards; drowning; electrocution; collapsing wall.
Considerations for transfer:
⚙ Welfare and security of the injured; size of the victim; physical capabilities of the rescuer; availability of resources; nature of the evacuation route; distance to be covered; gender.

EMERGENCY ACTION PRINCIPLES
⚙ 1. Survey the scene: Is it safe? What happened? How many are injured? Are there bystanders who can help?
⚙ 2. Do a primary survey of the victim (ABC checking): check responsiveness; if unresponsive, shout for help; position the victim if needed; open the airway; check breathing (if not breathing, give two initial ventilations); check pulse.
⚙ 3. Activate medical assistance.
⚙ 4. Do a secondary survey: interview the victim; check if breathing, pulse, and temperature are normal; look for other injuries.
Added: ABC means Airway, Breathing, Circulation.

PATIENT CATEGORIES AND TAGGING
★ Red (highest priority): needs immediate care and transfer. Airway and breathing problems, open chest or abdominal wounds, severe head injuries, tension pneumothorax, pericardial tamponade, impending shock.
★ Yellow (second priority): care and transport can be delayed for a while. Burns with complications, back injuries, major open fractures, eye and abdominal injuries.
★ Green (low priority): the "walking wounded", treated last. Simple fractures, sprains, soft tissue injuries, other non-serious injuries.
★ Black (lowest priority): near death or dead, devastating injuries, little chance of survival.
The module labels Yellow as "IMMEDIATE PRIORITY", but its description says care can be delayed temporarily. Yellow is the second priority, after Red.
The module writes "pre-cardial tamponade"; the correct term is pericardial tamponade (fluid squeezing the heart).
★ Triage: sorting victims by how urgently they need care.

FIRST AID
★ First aid: immediate care given to a person who has been injured or suddenly taken ill, when medical service is not available.
Objectives:
⚙ Alleviate human suffering and pain; prevent added injury or danger; prolong life.
Roles:
⚙ A bridge that fills the gap between the victim and the physician.
⚙ Not meant to compete with or replace the physician.
⚙ Ends when the physician's service begins.
Values of first aid training:
⚙ Preventing and caring for accidental injury or sudden illness.
⚙ Caring for persons caught in disasters.
⚙ Equipping people to deal with the whole situation, the person, and the injury.
⚙ Knowing what to do and what not to do.
Importance: it can make the difference between:
⚙ Life and death; temporary and permanent disability; rapid recovery and long hospitalization.
Do's:
⚙ Be calm and deliberate; keep onlookers away; make the victim comfortable and cheerful; keep handling of the victim to a minimum; loosen tight clothing.
Don'ts:
⚙ Give any liquid to an unconscious victim; try to arouse an unconscious victim; let the victim see their own injury.
Characteristics of a good first aider:
⚙ Gentle, resourceful, observant, tactful, empathetic.
Hindrances to giving first aid:
⚙ Unfavorable surroundings; presence of crowds; pressure from the victim or relatives.

COMMON MISTAKES
⚙ Swapping the percentages: 88% unsafe acts, 10% unsafe conditions, 2% non-preventable.
⚙ Running outside during an earthquake while indoors. Stay in and Duck, Cover, and Hold.
⚙ Using the elevator during an earthquake.
⚙ Mixing up the tags: Red first, Yellow second, Green walking wounded, Black near death.
⚙ Giving water to an unconscious victim.
⚙ Thinking first aid replaces the doctor. It ends when the physician's service begins.
