Lecture 3 The Human Being
Human capabilities and limitations: perception, memory and thinking (after Dix, Finlay, Abowd and Beale, 2005)

HCI STARTS WITH THE HUMAN
★ User: the central character in human-computer interaction (HCI).
We need to understand human capabilities.
We need to understand human limitations.
Human beings are complex.
The human is modelled as an information processor: input-output channels are the first part and memory is the second part.

INPUT-OUTPUT CHANNELS AND THE PERCEPTUAL SYSTEM
★ Perceptual system: sight, hearing, touch, taste and smell.
HCI is concerned with three of the five senses; taste and smell are not currently significant, which leaves sight, hearing and touch.
EASIER: for interface design, only the eyes, ears and skin matter right now.

SIGHT
★ Visual processing: transformation and interpretation of a complete image.
Expectations affect the way an image is perceived.
Visual processing compensates for movement, colour and brightness.
Visual processing helps resolve ambiguities.
★ Familiarity: a third cue for perceiving size (shown with an optical illusion picture).
★ Depth perception: another visual cue (shown with a landscape photograph).
Colour perception is another visual capability (introduced with pictures).

READING
★ Reading: perception and processing of text is a special case of visual perception.
⚙ Reading stage 1: the visual pattern of the word is perceived.
⚙ Reading stage 2: the word is decoded with an internal representation of the language.
⚙ Reading stage 3: syntactic and semantic analysis.
Adults read about 250 words per minute.
★ Word shape: words are recognised by their shape.
⚙ Rule: removing word-shape clues by using all caps is detrimental to reading speed and accuracy, so avoid all caps for text.
Meaning depends on context: Il can be the letters IL (as in Ilocano) or the roman number 2 (as in I, II, III, IV, V).
EASIER: the same shape is read differently depending on what surrounds it.
Example sentence shown: The quick brown fox jumps over the the lazy dog, with a repeated word across a line break.
★ Whole-word reading: text with scrambled middle letters (first and last letter in place) can still be read because we do not read every letter by itself but the word as a whole.

HEARING
★ Hearing: humans can hear sounds from 20 Hz to 15 kHz.
Humans can distinguish frequency changes of less than 1.5 Hz at low frequencies.
Hearing is less accurate at high frequencies.
Hearing can be selective.
Sound can convey a lot of information.

TOUCH
★ Haptic perception: an important means of feedback.
If we can see an object but not feel it, speed and accuracy of response are reduced.

HUMAN MEMORY
Memory is the second part of the model of the human as an information processor.
★ Three types of memory: sensory buffers, short-term memory and long-term memory.
★ Multistage model: sensory registers feed the short-term store, then short-term memory, then long-term memory, with executive control processes overseeing; data is lost from the system.
Timing: the short-term store lasts 0.5 to 2.0 seconds.
Timing: short-term memory lasts about 20 seconds.

SENSORY MEMORY
★ Iconic memory: visual; persistence of the image after the stimulus has been removed.
★ Echoic memory: aural; allows a brief play-back.
★ Haptic memory: touch.
Demonstration: an image (a chocolate cake) is flashed and removed, then viewers are asked what it was.
Pictures of a pillow and a cactus illustrate haptic memory.

SHORT-TERM MEMORY
★ Short-term memory: a scratch-pad for temporary recall, used for information needed fleetingly; rapid access, limited capacity.
⚙ Capacity: 7 +/- 2 chunks of information.
Patterns are useful memory aids.
EASIER: group items into meaningful chunks so more fits into the 7 +/- 2 slots.
Demonstration: memorise a picture for 10 seconds, write down everything remembered, then count how many you got.

LONG-TERM MEMORY
★ Long-term memory: stores everything we know: factual information, experiential knowledge and procedural rules of behaviour.
Capacity is huge, if not unlimited.
Access time is relatively slow (1/10 second).
Forgetting occurs more slowly.
Long-term memory has two parts: episodic and semantic.
Information can be organised into semantic networks, frames, scripts and production rules.
★ Episodic memory: memory of events in serial form; we can reconstruct actual events based on experience.
★ Semantic memory: structured record of facts, concepts and skills; derived from episodic memory.
EASIER: episodic is what happened to me, semantic is what I know.
Semantic memory prompts used: the national hero of the Philippines, the numeric value of pi, the colours of the rainbow.
★ Semantic networks: interconnections or associations among memories (for example, what you associate with the word cat).
★ Frames: contain slots or attributes; attributes represent default, fixed or variable information.
Frame example for Dog: fixed legs 4, default sound bark, variable size and colour.
EASIER: a frame is a form with some boxes pre-filled, some holding a usual answer, and some left open.
★ Scripts: represent default stereotypical information.
⚙ Script components: entry conditions, results, props, roles, scenes and tracks.
Script example: goal is to fill my mug with coffee; entry condition is that my mug is empty; result is that my mug is full of coffee.
Script example continued: props are mug, coffee machine and coffee; role is me making my coffee and filling my cup.
Script example continued: scenes are first preparing the coffee and then drinking it when ready; the track is no more coffee available.
★ Production rules: a series of condition-action (if-then) statements.
Production rule example: if it is raining then bring an umbrella.

LONG-TERM MEMORY PROCESSES
Long-term memory processes are storing, forgetting and remembering.
⚙ Total time hypothesis: the amount learned is proportional to the amount of time spent.
⚙ Distribution of practice effect: learning time is most effective if distributed over time.
EASIER: total time says more hours means more learning; distribution says spread those hours out.
⚙ Information must be meaningful for it to be stored.
Demonstration: an abstract word list (faith, age, cold, tenet, quiet, logic, idea, value, past, large) and a concrete word list (boat, tree, cat, child, rug, man, church, gun, flame, head) are each shown for 10 seconds.
⚙ Result: it is easier to remember concrete words than abstract words, so the second list is usually recalled better.
Forgetting has two causes: decay and interference.
★ Interference: old memory replaced by new or vice versa.
★ Retroactive interference: new information replaces the old.
★ Proactive inhibition: the old memory interferes with the new information.
EASIER: retroactive means the new thing pushes the old one out; proactive means the old thing blocks the new one.
Remembering (information retrieval) takes two forms.
★ Recall: information is reproduced from memory.
★ Recognition: the information has been seen before.
EASIER: recall is writing the answer from a blank page; recognition is spotting the right answer in a list.

THINKING
Thinking covers reasoning and problem solving.
★ Reasoning: the process of deriving new information from what is known.
★ Deductive reasoning: two or more assertions lead to a conclusion, with mathematical certainty.
Deductive example: all blondes are fun; Christy is a blonde; therefore Christy is fun.
★ Inductive reasoning: arriving at generalisations from observations we have seen about cases we have not seen.
Inductive example: every elephant we have seen has a trunk; therefore all elephants have trunks.
★ Abductive reasoning: formulation of hypotheses to explain a phenomenon.
EASIER: deduction proves, induction generalises, abduction proposes an explanation.

PROBLEM SOLVING
★ Problem solving: the process of finding a solution to an unfamiliar situation.
Three examples are covered: Gestalt, problem space theory and analogy in problem solving.
★ Gestalt: German for whole or form; people draw on previous experience, have insights, are sense-makers and restructure the problem.
Gestalt weakness: the theory lacked structure and support and does not explain insight and restructuring.
Gestalt principle: the whole is greater than the sum of its parts.
Gestalt principle: people perform based on understanding the general principles of a situation.
Gestalt warning: if we perform on memorised facts, we make stupid mistakes.
★ Problem space theory: the problem is represented in terms of problem states, and heuristics are employed to go from the initial state to the goal state.
Problem space theory limit: the general problem solver works for well-structured domains, but real-world problems are more complex.
★ Analogy in problem solving: interested in how people solve novel problems; similarities between a known domain and the new one are noted, and operators from the known domain are transferred.
★ Skill acquisition: the ability to remember larger and larger chunks, for example chess players.
Novices group problems according to superficial characteristics.
Experts group problems according to conceptual similarities.
