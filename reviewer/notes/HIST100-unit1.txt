Unit 1
The Meaning and Importance of History and Historical Sources

MEANING AND IMPORTANCE
★ History is an evidence-based interpretation of the past, built by studying surviving traces such as documents, photographs, laws, maps, artifacts, oral testimony, buildings, and digital records.
★ Dates establish sequence; sources provide evidence; analysis explains relationships, causes, significance, perspectives, and limitations.
★ History helps explain identity, society, power, communities, and institutions, and develops evidence-based thinking.
EASIER: Dates order the clues, sources provide clues, and analysis explains what the clues may mean.

THE W’S OF HISTORY
★ Who identifies participants and visible or missing voices. What identifies the event or process. When establishes chronology. Where supplies geographical and local context. Why asks causes and significance. How asks change, continuity, and supporting evidence.
★ An event is a specific happening at a time and place; a process is long-term change such as colonization or migration.
★ Before-event sources may show background; during-event sources may show immediate experience; later sources may reflect consequences or memory. Chronology prevents events from being mixed.
★ Historical narratives may preserve leaders and elites more visibly than women, Indigenous peoples, workers, and ordinary citizens.
EASIER: Ask who, what, when, where, why, and how.

THEORY LENSES
★ Great Man / Hero-centered: individuals and decisions; useful for leadership and biography, may overlook broader conditions.
★ Cyclical: recurring rise, growth, peak, crisis, breakdown, renewal; change may not repeat neatly.
★ Linear / Progress: forward development; useful for reforms, technology, institutions, may assume later is always better.
★ Materialist / Marxist: economy, class, ownership, labor, conflict; reveals inequality, may underplay culture, religion, choice.
★ Annales / Social History: everyday life and ordinary people, including work, food, belief, communities.
★ Postcolonial / Filipino-centered: colonial power, Filipino voices, bias, local meaning, resistance, agency, silenced views.
★ A theory lens emphasizes parts of an event; it should explain evidence, not replace it.
EASIER: Lenses focus attention differently; keep checking the evidence.

HISTORICAL SOURCES
★ Written sources (letters, laws, newspapers) offer statements, rules, arguments, accounts. Visual sources (photos, maps, posters) offer visual or spatial evidence.
★ Oral sources (interviews, testimonies) offer memory and spoken perspectives. Material sources (artifacts, buildings, clothing) offer physical clues about life, technology, culture, environment.
★ Digital sources (posts, videos, metadata) require checks of origin and alteration history.
★ Primary sources are from the period or directly connected to it: diaries, letters, photos, maps, laws, speeches, artifacts. They are not automatically accurate.
★ Secondary sources interpret primary sources: history books, journal articles, documentaries. Tertiary sources summarize: encyclopedias, timelines, glossaries.
★ Type depends on the research question. Philippine examples: Kartilya ng Katipunan (revolutionary ideals); 1898 Declaration (political claims); Rizal writings (nationalist thought); 1987 Constitution (legal-political framework); old photos/maps (visual-spatial evidence); oral testimony (memory and lived experience).
★ Fame, official status, and age do not remove the need to check authorship, context, purpose, provenance, and limitations. Match claims to what evidence can establish.

PROVENANCE, AUTHENTICITY, CREDIBILITY
★ Provenance is origin and preservation history: creator, date/place, audience, preservation, and path to the present. Unclear creator, date, archive, or transmission calls for caution.
★ Authenticity asks if a source is genuine or accurately reproduced. Physical clues: paper, ink, seal, signature. Digital: metadata, upload history, file origin. Content: language, dates, names. Expert: archive records and catalogs.
★ Appearance alone does not prove authenticity; an authentic source can still be biased, inaccurate, or limited.
★ Credibility asks whether claims are trustworthy for a specific question. Assess author knowledge/access/perspective, purpose (inform, persuade, justify, explain), and corroboration from other sources.
★ Provenance asks where from; authenticity asks genuine; credibility asks trustworthy for this question. Explain source limits, not just “reliable/unreliable.”
EASIER: A real source can still be mistaken or biased.

HISTORICAL CRITICISM AND THINKING SKILLS
★ Historical criticism judges authenticity, meaning, accuracy, and reliability.
★ External criticism checks origin/authenticity: authorship, date, place, form (original/copy/translation), condition (altered/damaged/edited), provenance.
★ Internal criticism checks what it says: wording, purpose, audience, bias, plausibility, and silences. A genuine source can be inaccurate or misleading.
★ Sourcing asks who made it and why; contextualizing asks what was happening; close reading asks what it says/shows; corroborating asks what other sources say.
⚙ Source it, contextualize it, read it closely, and compare it with other evidence.

SOURCE EVALUATION MATRIX
★ Rate provenance, authenticity, credibility separately as High/Medium/Low; usefulness as Strong/Limited. A source may have clear provenance but moderate credibility, or limited usefulness for a broad claim.
⚙ Evaluate each criterion separately and explain support and limitations.

EXAMPLE: HISTORICAL PHOTOGRAPH
⚙ Claim: “This photo proves the whole event.” Ask who took it, when and where, whether staged/cropped/captioned later, what is outside the frame, and whether written/oral sources corroborate it.
⚙ Match conclusion to evidence. One photo may support a limited claim about its frame but may not explain an entire event.
EASIER: A photo captures one angle and moment; check beyond the frame.

COMMON MISTAKES
★ Do not treat history as memorization alone, primary as automatically accurate, age/fame/official status as proof, authenticity as credibility, or one photo as proof of an entire event.
★ Do not classify without the research question, let theory replace evidence, ignore missing voices, or mix events without chronology.

EXAM TIPS
★ Identify the question; evaluate provenance, authenticity, credibility, usefulness. Keep external and internal criticism distinct. Use all six W’s; compare theory emphasis and limitation; corroborate broad claims.

SELF-TEST
Q: What are the three core source tests?
★ Provenance, authenticity, and credibility.
Q: What is the difference between event and process?
★ An event is a specific happening; a process is long-term change.
Q: What are the four historical thinking skills?
★ Sourcing, contextualizing, close reading, and corroborating.
