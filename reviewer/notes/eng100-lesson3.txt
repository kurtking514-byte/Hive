3 Local and Global Communication in Multicultural Settings
Understanding cultural diversity, intercultural sensitivity, and respectful language

MODULE OVERVIEW

★ Learning objectives: define cultural diversity; adapt cultural awareness and sensitivity; apply the Developmental Model of Intercultural Sensitivity; determine culturally appropriate terms.

CULTURE

★ Culture includes beliefs, values, norms, behaviors, and a person’s overall way of being.
EASIER: Culture is a guide people use to understand the world and interact.
★ Cultural diversity, synonymous with multiculturalism, recognizes cultural, racial, and ethnic differences, particularly those of minority groups, within a dominant political culture.
★ Cultural awareness means knowing multiple cultures exist and can have different attitudes and outlooks based on religion, ethnicity, nationality, and other factors.
★ Cultural sensitivity means accepting differences without insisting one’s culture is better or everyone should do things one’s way.
★ Ethnocentrism is the attitude that one’s own group, ethnicity, or nationality is superior to others.
★ Ethnorelativism is an acquired ability to see many values and behaviors as cultural rather than universal.
★ Cultural stereotypes rely on limited observed characteristics and often cause oversimplification and misinterpretation.
EASIER: Do not generalize about a group from a few observations.

COMMUNICATION STYLES

★ High-context communication conveys much meaning indirectly through context, relationships, shared experiences, tone, nonverbal cues, and what is left unsaid.
★ Low-context communication conveys meaning explicitly through clear, direct, specific words.
★ Cultures may tend higher or lower context, but individuals differ by personality, situation, profession, age, language proficiency, and relationship.
★ Multicultural communication occurs when people from different cultural backgrounds interact and exchange information.
⚙ In cross-cultural communication consider words, context, relationships, tone, nonverbal cues, and implied meaning.
⚙ Treat cultural tendencies as general examples, not as descriptions of every individual.
★ Generally more high-context examples: Japan, China, South Korea, Thailand, Indonesia, Philippines, India, Saudi Arabia / Arab cultures, Mexico, Italy, Spain, Brazil.
★ Generally more low-context examples: United States, Germany, Switzerland, Sweden, Canada, Australia, United Kingdom, Netherlands, Denmark, Norway, New Zealand, Austria.

CULTURAL DIVERSITY IN EDUCATION

★ Educational contributions listed: learning and growth, deep confidence, empathy, and preparation for the future.
★ Diversity benefits listed: new opportunities, compassion, innovation, productivity, and problem-solving.

DMIS

★ The Developmental Model of Intercultural Sensitivity (DMIS), developed by Dr. Milton Bennett, describes stages of intercultural sensitivity and competence as people engage across cultures.
★ Six stages: Denial, Defense, Minimization, Acceptance, Adaptation, Integration.
⚙ Memorize: Denial → Defense → Minimization → Acceptance → Adaptation → Integration.

GENDER-SENSITIVE LANGUAGE

★ Alternatives: mankind → people, humanity, or human beings; man-to-man defense → one-on-one defense; man the operation → staff the operation; manpower → labor or human resources; layman’s term → ordinary term.
★ Alternatives: chairman → chairperson; policeman → police officers; sales girl → sales clerk; spokesman → spokesperson; lady lawyer → lawyer.
★ Neutral examples: “Each student should hand in their term paper by tomorrow”; “Nurses care for their patients.”
★ “She shoots well” describes ability directly; “She shoots like a man” makes a gender comparison.
★ “A brilliant researcher” describes achievement without gender qualification.
★ Be respectful about a person’s gender, orientation, and sexual preference.
⚙ Use neutral pronouns where gender is unknown or irrelevant; describe skills directly rather than by gender.

AGE, RACE, RELIGION, AND DISABILITY

★ Ageist examples: “You’re too old to understand technology. Let the younger staff handle it” and “You’re too old to learn new things.”
★ Age-sensitive examples: “Your experience is valuable, and we can provide training and support to ensure you are comfortable with technology” and “People of all ages are capable of learning and adapting to new challenges.”
★ Race examples contrast “A Chinese national was caught” with “A man was caught,” and “She is pretty for an Aeta” with “She is pretty,” illustrating removal of irrelevant labels or qualifiers.
★ Respectful faith example: “Our club welcomes members of all faiths and backgrounds who share our interests,” instead of requiring one religious belief.
★ Respectful disability examples: “a child who has autism” and “a person who is visually impaired.”
⚙ Revise wording by identifying assumptions, checking relevance, removing unnecessary labels, and stating relevant information respectfully.
⚙ Replace age-based assumptions with wording recognizing experience and learning ability.
⚙ Welcome people of all faiths when membership is based on shared interests; use respectful disability wording.

EXAMPLE: REVISING WORDING

⚙ Step 1: Identify assumptions about gender, age, race, faith, or disability.
⚙ Step 2: Decide whether the information is relevant.
⚙ Step 3: Remove an unnecessary assumption or label.
⚙ Step 4: State relevant information respectfully and directly.
⚙ Example: Replace “You’re too old to learn new things” with “People of all ages are capable of learning and adapting to new challenges.”
⚙ Example: Replace “She shoots like a man” with “She shoots well.”

COMMON MISTAKES

★ Awareness recognizes differences; sensitivity accepts differences without claiming superiority.
★ Ethnocentrism claims superiority; ethnorelativism recognizes cultural variation.
★ High-context communication relies more on context and indirect cues; low-context communication uses explicit wording.
★ Country-level communication lists describe tendencies, not every individual.
★ Stereotypes generalize from limited observations and can misinterpret people.
★ Avoid irrelevant assumptions and labels about gender, age, race, religion, or disability.
