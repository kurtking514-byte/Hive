1.0 Logic
Propositions, Boolean connectives, truth tables, and logical equivalence

WHAT IS MATHEMATICAL LOGIC
Mathematical logic is a tool for working with complicated compound statements.
It includes a language for expressing compound statements.
It includes a concise notation for writing them.
It includes a methodology for objectively reasoning about their truth or falsity.
It is the foundation for expressing formal proofs in all branches of mathematics.
EASIER: Think of logic as the grammar and arithmetic of reasoning. Grammar tells you which sentences make sense, and arithmetic tells you how to work out whether they are true.
This module has two main parts: basic definitions, and equivalence rules and derivations.

WHAT IS PROPOSITIONAL LOGIC
★ Propositional logic is the logic of compound statements built from simpler statements using so-called Boolean connectives.
EASIER: Boolean connectives are little joining words such as NOT, AND, OR, and IF-THEN. They work like the plus and times of ordinary arithmetic, except that they combine true/false statements instead of numbers.
Some applications in computer science are the design of digital electronic circuits, the expression of conditions in programs, and queries to databases and search engines.
The two thinkers pictured with this topic are George Boole (1815-1864), after whom Boolean connectives are named, and Chrysippus of Soli (ca. 281 B.C. to 205 B.C.), an ancient Greek logician.

PROPOSITIONS
★ A proposition (usually named p, q, r, and so on) is a statement, meaning a declarative sentence, with a definite meaning and a truth value that is either true (T) or false (F), never both, never neither, and never somewhere in between.
EASIER: A proposition is a sentence that you could stamp with a big TRUE or a big FALSE. It is like a light switch that is either on or off, with no dimmer.
You might not know the actual truth value, and it might depend on the situation, but it must be exactly one of the two.
For now, think True/False only.
★ A compound proposition is a proposition built from simpler propositions using Boolean connectives.
Notation: T :≡ True and F :≡ False, where the symbol :≡ means is defined as.

EXAMPLES OF PROPOSITIONS
It is raining. This is a proposition in a given situation, because at any moment it is either true or false.
Beijing is the capital of China. This is a proposition (it is true).
1 + 2 = 3 is a proposition (it is true).
Added example: 1 + 2 = 5 is also a proposition. It is false, but it still has a definite truth value.

EXAMPLES OF NON-PROPOSITIONS
Who's there? is NOT a proposition. It is interrogative, meaning a question.
La la la la la is NOT a proposition. It is a meaningless interjection.
Just do it! is NOT a proposition. It is imperative, meaning a command.
Yeah, Way nas di, whatever... is NOT a proposition. It is vague and has no definite meaning.
1 + 2 is NOT a proposition. It is an expression whose value is the number 3, not true or false.
EASIER: Expression versus proposition. An expression such as 1 + 2 is like a noun phrase (it names a number). A proposition such as 1 + 2 = 3 is a full sentence that can be true or false.

THE SIX BOOLEAN OPERATORS
Negation operator: nickname NOT. It works on one proposition (unary). Its symbol is a short hook, written as a corner-shaped sign in textbooks.
Conjunction operator: nickname AND. It joins two propositions (binary). Its symbol is a wedge pointing up, like a capital A without its crossbar.
Disjunction operator: nickname OR. It joins two propositions. Its symbol is a wedge pointing down, like the letter v.
Exclusive-OR operator: nickname XOR. It joins two propositions. Its symbol is a plus sign inside a circle.
Implication operator: nickname IMPLIES. Its symbol is a single arrow, written -> in plain text.
Biconditional operator: nickname IFF (if and only if). Its symbol is a double-headed arrow, written <-> in plain text.
In this plain-text version, the words NOT, AND, OR, and XOR are used in place of the symbols.

THE NEGATION OPERATOR (NOT)
★ The unary negation operator NOT transforms a proposition into its logical negation, and NOT p always has the opposite truth value of p.
EASIER: NOT is a flip switch. True becomes false, and false becomes true.
Example: if p = I have brown hair, then NOT p = I do not have brown hair.
Truth table for NOT. The column holding p is the operand column. The column holding NOT p is the result column.
⚙ Row 1: p = T gives NOT p = F.
⚙ Row 2: p = F gives NOT p = T.

NEGATING AND, NEGATING OR (DE MORGAN'S LAWS)
Consider the statement I am both rich and happy.
For this statement to be false, I could be either not rich or not happy.
Let A = I am rich and B = I am happy.
★ The negation of A AND B is NOT A OR NOT B. In words: I am not rich or I am not happy.
★ The negation of A OR B is NOT A AND NOT B.
Summary: the negation of A or B is not A and not B. The negation of A and B is not A or not B.
EASIER: To negate a joined statement, push the NOT inside and swap AND with OR (or OR with AND). These two rules are called De Morgan's laws.
Added example: the statement I am rich or happy is false only when I am neither. So its negation is I am not rich and I am not happy.

THE CONJUNCTION OPERATOR (AND)
★ The binary conjunction operator AND combines two propositions to form their logical conjunction, and p AND q is true only when both p and q are true.
EASIER: AND is a strict teacher who says you pass only if you hand in the essay AND take the exam. Missing either one means you fail.
Example: if p = I will have salad for lunch and q = I will have steak for dinner, then p AND q = I will have salad for lunch and I will have steak for dinner.
Memory trick: the AND symbol points up like the letter A, and it means AND.
Truth table for AND. The columns for p and q are the operand columns.
⚙ Row 1: p = T, q = T gives p AND q = T.
⚙ Row 2: p = T, q = F gives p AND q = F.
⚙ Row 3: p = F, q = T gives p AND q = F.
⚙ Row 4: p = F, q = F gives p AND q = F.
⚙ Rule: a conjunction p1 AND p2 AND ... AND pn of n propositions has 2^n rows in its truth table.
⚙ Check: with n = 1 there are 2 rows, with n = 2 there are 4 rows, with n = 3 there are 8 rows, and with n = 5 there are 32 rows.
Also, the operations NOT and AND together are sufficient to express any Boolean truth table.
EASIER: Sufficient means that with only these two building blocks you can construct every other connective. Operator sets with this power are called universal.

THE DISJUNCTION OPERATOR (OR)
★ The binary disjunction operator OR combines two propositions to form their logical disjunction, and p OR q is true when p is true, or q is true, or both are true. It is false only when both are false.
Example: let p = My car has a bad engine and q = My car has a bad carburetor.
Then p OR q = Either my car has a bad engine, or my car has a bad carburetor.
The meaning is like and/or in English.
Memory trick: after the downward-pointing axe of the OR symbol splits the wood, you can take 1 piece OR the other, or both.
★ Because OR includes the possibility that both are true, it is also called inclusive or.
EASIER: Inclusive OR is like a menu that says you may have soup, or salad, or both. Having both is allowed.
Truth table for OR.
⚙ Row 1: p = T, q = T gives p OR q = T.
⚙ Row 2: p = T, q = F gives p OR q = T. This differs from AND.
⚙ Row 3: p = F, q = T gives p OR q = T. This differs from AND.
⚙ Row 4: p = F, q = F gives p OR q = F.
NOT and OR together are also universal.

NESTED PROPOSITIONAL EXPRESSIONS
⚙ Rule: use parentheses to group sub-expressions, so that it is clear which connective is applied to which pieces.
EASIER: Parentheses in logic work just like parentheses in arithmetic. (2 + 3) * 4 is different from 2 + 3 * 4, and the same kind of difference appears in logic.
Let f = I just saw my old friend, g = he's grown, and s = I've shrunk.
The sentence I just saw my old friend, and either he's grown or I've shrunk is written f AND (g OR s).
EXAMPLE: TRANSLATING A NESTED SENTENCE
⚙ Step 1: name the simple propositions: f, g, and s as above.
⚙ Step 2: find the main connective. The sentence says and either ..., so the main connective is AND, joining f with the whole phrase either he's grown or I've shrunk.
⚙ Step 3: the phrase either he's grown or I've shrunk is g OR s, and it must be grouped as one piece.
⚙ Answer: f AND (g OR s).
⚙ Check: if the grouping is dropped, (f AND g) OR s means something different, namely I saw my old friend and he's grown, or else I've shrunk.

A SIMPLE EXERCISE (TRANSLATING SYMBOLS TO ENGLISH)
Let p = It rained last night, q = The sprinklers came on last night, and r = The lawn was wet this morning.
EXAMPLE: TRANSLATE EACH EXPRESSION INTO ENGLISH
⚙ NOT p = It didn't rain last night.
⚙ r AND NOT p = The lawn was wet this morning, and it didn't rain last night.
⚙ NOT r OR p OR q = Either the lawn wasn't wet this morning, or it rained last night, or the sprinklers came on last night.
⚙ Note: in NOT r OR p OR q, the NOT applies only to r, and it is read first.
Added example: p AND q = It rained last night and the sprinklers came on last night.

THE EXCLUSIVE OR OPERATOR (XOR)
★ The binary exclusive-or operator XOR combines two propositions to form their logical exclusive or (some jokingly call it exjunction), and p XOR q is true when p is true or q is true, but not both.
Example: let p = I will earn an A in this course and q = I will drop this course.
Then p XOR q = I will either earn an A for this course, or I will drop it (but not both!).
This operation is called exclusive or because it excludes the possibility that both p and q are true.
EASIER: XOR is like a menu that says you may have cake OR ice cream as your dessert, but not both. Exactly one choice is allowed.
Truth table for XOR.
⚙ Row 1: p = T, q = T gives p XOR q = F.
⚙ Row 2: p = T, q = F gives p XOR q = T.
⚙ Row 3: p = F, q = T gives p XOR q = T.
⚙ Row 4: p = F, q = F gives p XOR q = F. This differs from OR only in the first row.
NOT and XOR together are NOT universal.
EASIER: XOR and OR differ in exactly one row, the row where both inputs are true.

THE IMPLICATION OPERATOR (IF-THEN)
★ The implication p -> q states that p implies q. In p -> q, the proposition p is called the antecedent and the proposition q is called the consequent.
Meaning: if p is true, then q is true; but if p is not true, then q could be either true or false.
Example: let p = You study hard and q = You will get a good grade.
Then p -> q = If you study hard, then you will get a good grade. (Otherwise, it could go either way.)
EASIER: An implication is a promise. The promise is broken only when you do the first part (study hard) and the second part (a good grade) does not happen.
⚙ Rule: p -> q is false only when p is true but q is not true.
⚙ Rule: p -> q does not say that p causes q.
⚙ Rule: p -> q does not require that p or q are ever true.
Example: (1 = 0) -> pigs can fly is TRUE, because the antecedent is false.
Truth table for implication.
⚙ Row 1: p = T, q = T gives p -> q = T.
⚙ Row 2: p = T, q = F gives p -> q = F. This is the only false case.
⚙ Row 3: p = F, q = T gives p -> q = T.
⚙ Row 4: p = F, q = F gives p -> q = T.
EASIER: When the antecedent is false, the promise was never tested, so it cannot be broken. Logicians then count the implication as true.

EXAMPLES OF IMPLICATIONS
Decide whether each implication is true or false.
EXAMPLE: IF THIS LECTURE ENDS, THEN THE SUN WILL RISE TOMORROW
⚙ The antecedent (the lecture ends) is true and the consequent (the sun rises tomorrow) is true.
⚙ Row T, T of the truth table gives T.
⚙ Answer: True.
EXAMPLE: IF TUESDAY IS A DAY OF THE WEEK, THEN I AM A PENGUIN
⚙ The antecedent is true. The consequent (I am a penguin) is false.
⚙ Row T, F of the truth table gives F.
⚙ Answer: False.
EXAMPLE: IF 1+1=6, THEN R. DUTERTE WAS PHIL-PRESIDENT
⚙ The antecedent 1 + 1 = 6 is false, so the implication is true whatever the consequent is.
⚙ Here the consequent is also true, because Rodrigo Duterte was President of the Philippines. That is row F, T, which gives T.
⚙ Answer: True.
EXAMPLE: IF THE MOON IS MADE OF GREEN CHEESE, THEN I AM RICHER THAN BILL GATES
⚙ The antecedent is false. The consequent is also false.
⚙ Row F, F of the truth table gives T.
⚙ Answer: True.

CONVERSE, INVERSE, CONTRAPOSITIVE
★ For an implication p -> q, its converse is q -> p.
★ For an implication p -> q, its inverse is NOT p -> NOT q.
★ For an implication p -> q, its contrapositive is NOT q -> NOT p.
★ Exactly one of these three has the same meaning (the same truth table) as p -> q: the contrapositive.
EASIER: Converse means swap the two parts. Inverse means negate both parts but keep the order. Contrapositive means swap AND negate both parts.
Added example: for If you study hard, then you will get a good grade, the converse is If you get a good grade, then you study hard.
Added example: the inverse is If you do not study hard, then you will not get a good grade.
Added example: the contrapositive is If you do not get a good grade, then you did not study hard.
EXAMPLE: PROVING THAT P -> Q AND ITS CONTRAPOSITIVE ARE EQUIVALENT
⚙ Step 1: list all four rows for p and q.
⚙ Step 2: compute NOT q and NOT p, then compute p -> q and NOT q -> NOT p in every row.
⚙ Row 1: p = T, q = T, NOT q = F, NOT p = F, p -> q = T, NOT q -> NOT p = (F -> F) = T.
⚙ Row 2: p = T, q = F, NOT q = T, NOT p = F, p -> q = F, NOT q -> NOT p = (T -> F) = F.
⚙ Row 3: p = F, q = T, NOT q = F, NOT p = T, p -> q = T, NOT q -> NOT p = (F -> T) = T.
⚙ Row 4: p = F, q = F, NOT q = T, NOT p = T, p -> q = T, NOT q -> NOT p = (T -> T) = T.
⚙ Step 3: compare the two result columns. Both read T, F, T, T in the four rows, so they match in every row.
⚙ Conclusion: p -> q and its contrapositive are logically equivalent.
Added example: the converse q -> p has results T, T, F, T in the four rows, and the inverse NOT p -> NOT q also has T, T, F, T. They match each other, but neither matches p -> q, which is T, F, T, T. So the converse and the inverse are not equivalent to the original.

THE BICONDITIONAL OPERATOR (IFF)
★ The biconditional p <-> q states that p is true if and only if (IFF) q is true, so p and q have the same truth value.
Example: let p = Candidate X wins the election and q = Candidate X takes office after the election.
Then p <-> q = If, and only if, Candidate X wins the election, Candidate X takes office after the election.
EASIER: IFF is a two-way street. The first part guarantees the second, and the second guarantees the first.
★ The truth table of p <-> q is the exact opposite of the truth table of XOR, so p <-> q means NOT (p XOR q).
⚙ Rule: p <-> q does not imply that p and q are true, and it does not say that p and q cause each other.
Truth table for the biconditional.
⚙ Row 1: p = T, q = T gives p <-> q = T.
⚙ Row 2: p = T, q = F gives p <-> q = F.
⚙ Row 3: p = F, q = T gives p <-> q = F.
⚙ Row 4: p = F, q = F gives p <-> q = T.
Note that two false statements still give T, because they have the same truth value.

BOOLEAN OPERATIONS SUMMARY
We have seen 1 unary operator (out of the 4 possible) and 5 binary operators (out of the 16 possible). Their truth tables are below.
⚙ Row 1: p = T, q = T gives NOT p = F, p AND q = T, p OR q = T, p XOR q = F, p -> q = T, p <-> q = T.
⚙ Row 2: p = T, q = F gives NOT p = F, p AND q = F, p OR q = T, p XOR q = T, p -> q = F, p <-> q = F.
⚙ Row 3: p = F, q = T gives NOT p = T, p AND q = F, p OR q = T, p XOR q = T, p -> q = T, p <-> q = F.
⚙ Row 4: p = F, q = F gives NOT p = T, p AND q = F, p OR q = F, p XOR q = F, p -> q = T, p <-> q = T.

SOME ALTERNATIVE NOTATIONS
Different fields write the same connectives in different ways.
Propositional logic uses the symbols for NOT, AND, OR, XOR, implies, and iff described earlier.
Boolean algebra writes NOT p as p with a bar over it, p AND q as pq (written side by side), p OR q as p + q, and XOR with the circled plus sign.
C, C++, and Java word-wise operators are: ! for NOT, && for AND, || for OR, != for XOR, and == for iff.
C, C++, and Java bitwise operators are: ~ for NOT, & for AND, | for OR, and ^ for XOR.
Logic gates (in electronic circuits) have a NOT gate, an AND gate, an OR gate, and an XOR gate.
Added example: in a program, the condition (x > 0) && (y > 0) is true only when both parts are true, just like p AND q.

LOGICAL EQUIVALENCE
★ A tautology is a compound proposition that is true in every row of its truth table.
★ Compound proposition p is logically equivalent to compound proposition q, written p <=> q, if and only if the compound proposition p <-> q is a tautology.
★ Equivalently, p and q are logically equivalent exactly when they contain the same truth values in all rows of their truth tables.
EASIER: Two propositions are equivalent when they are twins. In every possible situation they give the same answer.
EASIER: The symbol <-> is a connective that builds a new proposition. The symbol <=> is a claim that two propositions always match, so it is a statement about them.
Added example: a contradiction is the opposite of a tautology. It is false in every row, such as p AND NOT p.
⚙ Steps for proving equivalence with truth tables: Step 1: list every combination of T and F for the simple propositions.
⚙ Step 2: compute a column for each sub-expression, working from the inside out.
⚙ Step 3: compute the final column for each of the two propositions.
⚙ Step 4: compare the two final columns in every row. If they match in all rows, they are equivalent. If they differ in even one row, they are not.

EXAMPLE: PROVE THAT P OR Q IS EQUIVALENT TO NOT (NOT P AND NOT Q)
⚙ Row 1: p = T, q = T: p OR q = T, NOT p = F, NOT q = F, NOT p AND NOT q = F, NOT (NOT p AND NOT q) = T.
⚙ Row 2: p = T, q = F: p OR q = T, NOT p = F, NOT q = T, NOT p AND NOT q = F, NOT (NOT p AND NOT q) = T.
⚙ Row 3: p = F, q = T: p OR q = T, NOT p = T, NOT q = F, NOT p AND NOT q = F, NOT (NOT p AND NOT q) = T.
⚙ Row 4: p = F, q = F: p OR q = F, NOT p = T, NOT q = T, NOT p AND NOT q = T, NOT (NOT p AND NOT q) = F.
⚙ The columns for p OR q and for NOT (NOT p AND NOT q) both read T, T, T, F.
⚙ Conclusion: p OR q is logically equivalent to NOT (NOT p AND NOT q).
EASIER: This shows why NOT and AND together are enough to build OR.
Added example: p -> q is equivalent to NOT p OR q. Check: Row T, T gives F OR T = T. Row T, F gives F OR F = F. Row F, T gives T OR T = T. Row F, F gives T OR F = T. This matches T, F, T, T for p -> q.

EXERCISES WITH SOLUTIONS
EXAMPLE: WRITE THE TRUTH TABLE FOR (P OR Q) -> R
⚙ Step 1: list all 2^3 = 8 rows for P, Q, R.
⚙ Step 2: compute the column P OR Q, then compute (P OR Q) -> R.
⚙ Row 1: P = T, Q = T, R = T: P OR Q = T, (P OR Q) -> R = T.
⚙ Row 2: P = T, Q = T, R = F: P OR Q = T, (P OR Q) -> R = F.
⚙ Row 3: P = T, Q = F, R = T: P OR Q = T, (P OR Q) -> R = T.
⚙ Row 4: P = T, Q = F, R = F: P OR Q = T, (P OR Q) -> R = F.
⚙ Row 5: P = F, Q = T, R = T: P OR Q = T, (P OR Q) -> R = T.
⚙ Row 6: P = F, Q = T, R = F: P OR Q = T, (P OR Q) -> R = F.
⚙ Row 7: P = F, Q = F, R = T: P OR Q = F, (P OR Q) -> R = T.
⚙ Row 8: P = F, Q = F, R = F: P OR Q = F, (P OR Q) -> R = T.
⚙ Answer: the final column reads T, F, T, F, T, F, T, T.
EXAMPLE: SHOW THAT NOT (P OR Q) IS EQUIVALENT TO NOT P AND NOT Q
⚙ Row 1: P = T, Q = T: P OR Q = T, NOT (P OR Q) = F, NOT P = F, NOT Q = F, NOT P AND NOT Q = F.
⚙ Row 2: P = T, Q = F: P OR Q = T, NOT (P OR Q) = F, NOT P = F, NOT Q = T, NOT P AND NOT Q = F.
⚙ Row 3: P = F, Q = T: P OR Q = T, NOT (P OR Q) = F, NOT P = T, NOT Q = F, NOT P AND NOT Q = F.
⚙ Row 4: P = F, Q = F: P OR Q = F, NOT (P OR Q) = T, NOT P = T, NOT Q = T, NOT P AND NOT Q = T.
⚙ Answer: both columns read F, F, F, T, so they are equivalent. This is De Morgan's law for OR.
EXAMPLE: CONSTRUCT A TRUTH TABLE FOR (NOT P AND Q) OR (P AND NOT Q)
⚙ Row 1: P = T, Q = T: NOT P = F, NOT P AND Q = F, NOT Q = F, P AND NOT Q = F, whole expression = F.
⚙ Row 2: P = T, Q = F: NOT P = F, NOT P AND Q = F, NOT Q = T, P AND NOT Q = T, whole expression = T.
⚙ Row 3: P = F, Q = T: NOT P = T, NOT P AND Q = T, NOT Q = F, P AND NOT Q = F, whole expression = T.
⚙ Row 4: P = F, Q = F: NOT P = T, NOT P AND Q = F, NOT Q = T, P AND NOT Q = F, whole expression = F.
⚙ Answer: the final column reads F, T, T, F. This is exactly the truth table of P XOR Q.
EXAMPLE: SHOW THAT NOT (P AND Q) IS EQUIVALENT TO NOT P OR NOT Q
⚙ Row 1: P = T, Q = T: P AND Q = T, NOT (P AND Q) = F, NOT P = F, NOT Q = F, NOT P OR NOT Q = F.
⚙ Row 2: P = T, Q = F: P AND Q = F, NOT (P AND Q) = T, NOT P = F, NOT Q = T, NOT P OR NOT Q = T.
⚙ Row 3: P = F, Q = T: P AND Q = F, NOT (P AND Q) = T, NOT P = T, NOT Q = F, NOT P OR NOT Q = T.
⚙ Row 4: P = F, Q = F: P AND Q = F, NOT (P AND Q) = T, NOT P = T, NOT Q = T, NOT P OR NOT Q = T.
⚙ Answer: both columns read F, T, T, T, so they are equivalent. This is De Morgan's law for AND.
EXAMPLE: SHOW THAT P AND (Q AND R) IS EQUIVALENT TO (P AND Q) AND R
⚙ Row 1: P = T, Q = T, R = T: Q AND R = T, P AND (Q AND R) = T, P AND Q = T, (P AND Q) AND R = T.
⚙ Row 2: P = T, Q = T, R = F: Q AND R = F, P AND (Q AND R) = F, P AND Q = T, (P AND Q) AND R = F.
⚙ Row 3: P = T, Q = F, R = T: Q AND R = F, P AND (Q AND R) = F, P AND Q = F, (P AND Q) AND R = F.
⚙ Row 4: P = T, Q = F, R = F: Q AND R = F, P AND (Q AND R) = F, P AND Q = F, (P AND Q) AND R = F.
⚙ Row 5: P = F, Q = T, R = T: Q AND R = T, P AND (Q AND R) = F, P AND Q = F, (P AND Q) AND R = F.
⚙ Row 6: P = F, Q = T, R = F: Q AND R = F, P AND (Q AND R) = F, P AND Q = F, (P AND Q) AND R = F.
⚙ Row 7: P = F, Q = F, R = T: Q AND R = F, P AND (Q AND R) = F, P AND Q = F, (P AND Q) AND R = F.
⚙ Row 8: P = F, Q = F, R = F: Q AND R = F, P AND (Q AND R) = F, P AND Q = F, (P AND Q) AND R = F.
⚙ Answer: both columns read T, F, F, F, F, F, F, F, so they are equivalent. In words, the grouping of a chain of ANDs does not matter.
EXAMPLE: SHOW THAT P -> Q IS NOT EQUIVALENT TO ITS CONVERSE Q -> P
⚙ Row 1: P = T, Q = T: P -> Q = T, Q -> P = T.
⚙ Row 2: P = T, Q = F: P -> Q = F, Q -> P = T.
⚙ Row 3: P = F, Q = T: P -> Q = T, Q -> P = F.
⚙ Row 4: P = F, Q = F: P -> Q = T, Q -> P = T.
⚙ Answer: the columns differ in Row 2 and Row 3, so P -> Q and Q -> P are not logically equivalent.

COMMON MISTAKES
Treating an expression such as 1 + 2 as a proposition. It has no truth value.
Thinking OR means exactly one. Ordinary OR is inclusive and is true when both are true. Only XOR excludes both.
Thinking p -> q is false whenever p is false. It is actually true whenever p is false.
Thinking p -> q means p causes q. It does not.
Treating the converse or inverse as equivalent to the original implication. Only the contrapositive is equivalent.
Negating A AND B as NOT A AND NOT B. The correct negation is NOT A OR NOT B.
Forgetting that n propositions give 2^n rows, not 2n rows.
Mixing up the connective <-> (it builds a proposition) with the claim <=> (it says two propositions are equivalent).

EXAM TIPS
List the rows in a fixed order (TT, TF, FT, FF for two letters) so you never miss or repeat a row.
Add one column for every sub-expression instead of working the whole thing in your head.
To prove equivalence, compare the final columns in every row. To disprove it, one differing row is enough.
Memorize that implication is false only in the row T, F.
For a negation of AND or OR, push NOT inside and swap AND with OR.

SELF-TEST
Q: What is a proposition?
★ A declarative sentence with a definite meaning whose truth value is exactly true or false, never both and never neither.
Q: How many rows does the truth table of a compound proposition with 4 different simple propositions have?
★ 2^4 = 16 rows.
Q: When is p OR q false?
★ Only when both p and q are false.
Q: When is p XOR q true?
★ When exactly one of p and q is true, but not both.
Q: When is p -> q false?
★ Only when p is true and q is false.
Q: What are the converse, inverse, and contrapositive of p -> q, and which one is equivalent to p -> q?
★ Converse: q -> p. Inverse: NOT p -> NOT q. Contrapositive: NOT q -> NOT p. The contrapositive is the equivalent one.
Q: What is the negation of A AND B?
★ NOT A OR NOT B.
Q: What does p <-> q mean in terms of XOR?
★ p <-> q means NOT (p XOR q), so p and q have the same truth value.
