OBJECTIVES
⚙ Understand the concept of World Englishes.
⚙ Identify and differentiate between various varieties of the English language.
⚙ Recognize the impact of language varieties on communication.

ENGLISH AS A LINGUA FRANCA
★ Lingua franca: a common language used by people who speak different first languages so they can communicate. English is the world's main lingua franca.
⚙ The lesson links English to language skills, grammar, communication, linguistics, and education.

WORLD ENGLISHES
★ World Englishes: the differences in the English language that emerge as it is used in various contexts across the world.
⚙ Examples named in the lesson: Philippine English, Canadian English, Australian English, American English, Indian English, British English, Ugandan English.
EASIER: There is not just one English. Each country that uses it shapes its own words, spelling, and accent.

KACHRU'S CIRCLES OF ENGLISH
★ Kachru's Expanding Circles of English: a model that groups countries into three circles by how English is used there.
★ Inner Circle: countries where English is the native, first language. USA, UK, Canada, Australia, New Zealand.
★ Outer Circle: countries where English is a second or official language, usually from colonial history. Philippines, Bangladesh, Ghana, India, Kenya, Malaysia, Nigeria, Pakistan, Singapore, Sri Lanka, Tanzania, Zambia.
★ Expanding Circle: countries where English is learned as a foreign language. China, Egypt, Indonesia, Israel, Korea, Nepal, Japan, Saudi Arabia, Taiwan, Russia, Zimbabwe.
Added example: the descriptions "native", "second or official", and "foreign language" are the usual meaning of each circle; the slide lists only the countries.
Difference: the Philippines is in the Outer Circle, not the Inner Circle. English is official there but is not the first language of most Filipinos.

AMERICAN ENGLISH
★ American English: also called United States English or U.S. English. It is the set of varieties of English native to the United States and widely adopted in Canada.

BRITISH ENGLISH
★ British English: English as spoken and written in the United Kingdom or, more broadly, throughout the British Isles.
⚙ Slight regional variations exist in formal, written English in the United Kingdom.

AMERICAN VS BRITISH ENGLISH
They differ in three areas:
⚙ Vocabulary
⚙ Spelling
⚙ Pronunciation

VOCABULARY DIFFERENCES
American word, then British word:
⚙ French fries: chips
⚙ Cotton candy: candyfloss
⚙ Apartment: flat
⚙ Garbage: rubbish
⚙ Cookie: biscuit
⚙ Parking lot: car park
⚙ Pants: trousers
⚙ Windshield: windscreen

SPELLING DIFFERENCES
American spelling, then British spelling:
⚙ -or (color, armor, flavor): -our (colour, armour, flavour)
⚙ -ize (apologize, fantasize, idolize): -ise (apologise, fantasise, idolise)
⚙ -yze (analyze, paralyze): -yse (analyse, paralyse)
⚙ Single L (traveled, labeling): double LL (travelled, labelling)
⚙ E (leukemia, pediatrics): AE (leukaemia, paediatrics)
⚙ -ense (defense): -ence (defence)
⚙ -og (dialog, catalog): -ogue (dialogue, catalogue)
⚙ -er (meter, liter): -re (metre, litre)
From the video (American, then British and Australian):
⚙ Check: cheque
⚙ Gray: grey
⚙ Tire: tyre
⚙ Jewelry: jewellery
⚙ Aluminum: aluminium
⚙ Mustache: moustache
⚙ Airplane: aeroplane
⚙ Pajamas: pyjamas
EASIER: British spelling usually keeps the older, longer form (colour, centre, catalogue, travelled). American spelling is usually shorter (color, center, catalog, traveled).

PRONUNCIATION DIFFERENCES
American, then British:
⚙ "Dance": /æ/ as in "dæns": /ɑː/ as in "dɑːns".
⚙ "Car": rhotic, the R is pronounced (/kɑr/): non-rhotic, the R is dropped (/kɑː/).
⚙ "Lot": /ɑ/ as in "lɑt": /ɒ/ as in "lɒt".
⚙ "Water": the T sounds like a soft D (/t/ as in "wɑdər"): glottal stop /ʔ/ as in "wɔːʔə".
⚙ "Herb": "ɜːrb", without the H: /h/ as in "hɜːrb", with the H.
⚙ "Schedule": /skɛdʒuːl/ ("skedjool"): /ʃɛdjuːl/ ("shedjool").
⚙ "Aluminum": /əˈluːmɪnəm/ ("uh-loo-muh-num"): "aluminium" /əˈljuːmɪniəm/ ("uh-lyoo-min-ee-um").
★ Rhotic: the R after a vowel is pronounced, as in American English. Non-rhotic: that R is not pronounced, as in British English.
Note: the slide places "hɜːrb" (with H) under American and "ɜːrb" under British. It is the other way around: Americans usually drop the H, and the British pronounce it.

AUSTRALIAN ENGLISH
★ Australian English: a major variety of English used throughout Australia.
⚙ English has no official status in Australia's constitution, but Australian English is the country's national and de facto official language because it is the first language of the majority of the population.
⚙ The video ("Sweater or Jumper?") compares the same words in American, British, and Australian English; see the lists above (lollies, runners, capsicum, hot chips, undies).
Added example: "de facto" means "in practice" even though it is not written into law.

PHILIPPINE ENGLISH
★ Philippine English: any variety of English (similar and related to American English) native to the Philippines, including that used by the media and the vast majority of educated Filipinos.
⚙ English was introduced to the Philippines by American colonizers.
⚙ At least 40 Filipino words have entered the Oxford English Dictionary (OED).

EXAMPLE: CR OR TOILET?
The video compares words used in Singapore, the Philippines, India, and the UK or US.
⚙ Toilet: Singapore "toilet"; Philippines "CR" (comfort room); India "toilet" or "washroom"; UK "loo".
⚙ Eggplant: Singapore "brinjal" or "eggplant"; Philippines "eggplant"; India "brinjal"; UK "aubergine".
⚙ Gas station: Singapore "petrol station"; Philippines "gas station"; India "petrol pump".
⚙ Mobile phone: Singapore "handphone"; Philippines "cellphone"; India "phone", "smartphone", or "handphone".
⚙ Cinema: Singapore "cinema"; Philippines and US "movie theater"; India "cinema hall"; UK "theatre".
⚙ Roundabout: Philippines "rotonda"; US "rotary"; Singapore "roundabout".

PHILIPPINE WORDS IN THE OXFORD ENGLISH DICTIONARY
Words added on June 25, 2015:
⚙ balikbayan, barangay, barkada, carnap, despedida, gimmick, halo-halo, high blood, KKB, presidentiable, sari-sari store, utang na loob
Words and meanings shown in the video:
★ Baon: money, food, or other provisions taken to school, work, or on a journey.
★ Bongga: flamboyant or extravagant; excellent or great.
★ Kikay: a flirtatious girl or woman; a girl or woman interested in beauty products and fashion.
★ Kilig: a feeling of exhilaration or elation caused by an exciting or romantic experience.
★ Kuya: an elder brother; a polite title or form of address for elder men.
★ Pasalubong: a gift or souvenir given to a friend or relative by a person who has returned from a trip or arrived for a visit.
★ Pulutan: food or snacks provided as an accompaniment to alcoholic drinks.
★ Suki: a buyer or seller in an arrangement where a customer regularly buys from the same provider in exchange for favourable treatment.
★ Holdupper: a person who commits a robbery using threats or violence.
★ Yaya: a woman employed by a family to look after a child, or a sick or elderly person.
⚙ Other Filipino words shown in the video: bahala na, buko juice, carnapper, dirty kitchen, estafa, mani-pedi, pan de sal, Mabuhay, kikay kit.
★ Gigil (later OED addition): an intense feeling caused by anger, eagerness, or the pleasure of seeing someone or something cute, often shown by clenched hands, gritted teeth, trembling, or pinching or squeezing the cute thing.
Difference: kilig is the giddy feeling from romance. Gigil is the urge to squeeze or pinch something cute, or an intense feeling from anger.

EXAMPLE: TOOTHPASTE OR COLGATE?
⚙ The video compares American English and Filipino English words for everyday things.
⚙ Many Filipinos call any toothpaste "Colgate", a brand name used as the general word.
⚙ Slippers: the Filipino speaker calls rubber sandals "slippers"; the American calls them "flip flops" and uses "slippers" for soft indoor shoes.
⚙ Other everyday items compared in the video: sneakers, pens, washcloths, margarine, and snacks.
Added example: using a brand name for the whole product type is also seen in "Colgate" for toothpaste and "Xerox" for photocopy.

IMPACT ON COMMUNICATION
⚙ The same object can have different names in different varieties, so a word that is normal in one country can confuse listeners from another (CR, loo, washroom).
⚙ The same word can mean different things (pants in the US versus the UK).
⚙ Spelling should stay consistent with one variety in a single piece of writing.
Added example: an American asking a British friend for "pants" may get underwear instead of trousers.

COMMON MISTAKES
⚙ Putting the Philippines in the Inner Circle. It is in the Outer Circle.
⚙ Mixing up the Outer Circle (second or official language, such as India and Singapore) with the Expanding Circle (foreign language, such as China, Japan, and Korea).
⚙ Swapping the spellings: colour, travelled, defence, and catalogue are British; color, traveled, defense, and catalog are American.
⚙ Thinking British English is rhotic. American English pronounces the R in "car"; British English drops it.
⚙ Confusing kilig with gigil.
⚙ Forgetting that Philippine English is similar and related to American English, not British English.
