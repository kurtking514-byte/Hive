OVERVIEW
⚙ The module introduces three foundational types of fitness training: aerobic, strength, and flexibility.
⚙ It shows how they support physical health, mental well-being, and academic performance.
★ MMFIT: Motivation, Mode, Frequency, Intensity, Time. It is used to personalize a fitness routine.

LESSON 1: AEROBIC TRAINING
Fuel for the heart and brain.
★ Aerobic exercise: any continuous, rhythmic physical activity that increases your heart rate, boosts breathing rate, engages large muscle groups (especially the legs and core), and relies on oxygen to produce energy over an extended period.
★ "Aerobic" literally means "with oxygen".
⚙ During aerobic activity, the body uses oxygen to convert carbohydrates and fats into energy, so movement can last longer.

HOW AEROBIC TRAINING HELPS THE HEART
⚙ Strengthens the cardiac muscle, improving its ability to pump blood efficiently.
⚙ Lowers resting heart rate and blood pressure, reducing the risk of heart disease.
⚙ Improves circulation, delivering oxygen and nutrients to tissues more effectively.

HOW AEROBIC TRAINING HELPS THE BRAIN
⚙ Increases blood flow to the brain, enhancing memory, focus, and cognitive function.
⚙ Stimulates the release of endorphins and dopamine, which elevate mood and reduce stress.
⚙ Supports neuroplasticity, helping the brain adapt and form new connections, which helps learning and mental health.
★ Neuroplasticity: the brain's ability to adapt and form new connections.

WHY AEROBIC TRAINING MATTERS TO STUDENTS
⚙ Mental clarity: helps with focus, memory, and academic performance.
⚙ Emotional regulation: reduces anxiety and depression symptoms.
⚙ Physical stamina: increases energy for daily tasks and reduces fatigue.
⚙ Weight management: burns calories and supports metabolic health.
⚙ Social connection: group aerobic activities foster community and motivation.

AEROBIC MMFIT GUIDELINES
⚙ Motivation: often driven by stress relief or social enjoyment.
⚙ Mode: aerobic activities like walking or dancing.
⚙ Intensity: moderate to vigorous (RPE 4 to 8).
⚙ Frequency: 3 to 5 times per week.
⚙ Time: 20 to 60 minutes per session.

RATE OF PERCEIVED EXERTION (RPE)
★ Rate of Perceived Exertion (RPE): a simple tool to measure how hard you feel you are working during physical activity. It is based on your personal perception, not on heart rate monitors or fitness trackers.
⚙ RPE 0, rest: no movement at all.
⚙ RPE 1 to 2, very light: gentle stretching or slow walking.
⚙ RPE 3 to 4, light: brisk walking, light dancing.
⚙ RPE 5 to 6, moderate: jogging, Zumba, bodyweight exercises.
⚙ RPE 7 to 8, hard: circuit training, Tabata.
⚙ RPE 9 to 10, maximal: sprinting, intense HIIT.
Why RPE matters for students and non-athletes:
⚙ Self-awareness: helps learners tune into their bodies and avoid overexertion.
⚙ Adaptability: lets students adjust intensity based on energy, mood, or fitness level.
⚙ Goal setting: supports MMFIT planning by matching effort to motivation and time.
⚙ Safety: reduces risk of injury by encouraging gradual progression.
EASIER: RPE is your own 0 to 10 rating of how hard the exercise feels. 0 is resting and 10 is all-out.

LESSON 2: STRENGTH TRAINING
Building power and resilience.
★ Strength training: also called resistance training. It involves exercises that make your muscles work against a form of resistance: body weight, gravity, resistance bands, or external weights.
⚙ Goal: increase muscular strength, endurance, and stability, which are essential for physical health and functional independence.

KEY CONCEPTS OF STRENGTH TRAINING
★ Uses resistance: resistance challenges your muscles, prompting them to adapt and grow stronger over time.
⚙ Bodyweight resistance, such as push-ups and squats.
⚙ External tools, such as dumbbells and resistance bands.
⚙ Environmental factors, such as gravity during wall sits or stair climbing.
⚙ For students: being able to carry a backpack, open a heavy door, or sit upright for long periods without fatigue.
★ Builds muscle: strength training stimulates muscle fibers to repair and grow, increasing muscle mass and tone.
⚙ More muscle mass also boosts metabolism, helping regulate weight and energy levels.
⚙ For non-athletes: you don't need to "bulk up"; even light resistance can improve tone, posture, and endurance.
★ Improves bone density: resistance training places stress on bones, which stimulates bone-building cells.
⚙ This is important for preventing osteoporosis and keeping the skeleton strong with age.
⚙ For students: strong bones support better posture and reduce the risk of injury from falls or accidents.
★ Enhances functional strength: being strong in ways that matter for daily life.
⚙ Lifting groceries, climbing stairs, getting up from the floor or a chair, carrying books or equipment.
Added example: osteoporosis is a condition where bones become weak and brittle and break more easily.

WHY STRENGTH TRAINING MATTERS TO STUDENTS
⚙ Postural support: reduces back and neck pain from prolonged sitting or screen time.
⚙ Injury prevention: strengthens muscles and joints, reducing the risk of strains or falls.
⚙ Confidence and body awareness: enhances self-image and movement control.
⚙ Energy and endurance: improves stamina for academic, work, or household tasks.
⚙ Accessibility: can be done at home with minimal equipment and modifications.

STRENGTH MMFIT GUIDELINES
⚙ Motivation: functional independence and body confidence.
⚙ Mode: resistance training using bodyweight or light tools.
⚙ Intensity: moderate to high (RPE 5 to 8).
⚙ Frequency: 2 to 3 times per week.
⚙ Time: 20 to 45 minutes per session.

LESSON 3: FLEXIBILITY TRAINING
Mobility for life.
★ Flexibility training: exercises that stretch muscles and soft tissues to increase the range of motion (ROM) around joints and reduce muscle stiffness.
⚙ It helps the body move more freely, comfortably, and efficiently when reaching, bending, walking, or sitting for long periods.

KEY CONCEPTS OF FLEXIBILITY TRAINING
★ Stretching muscles: stretching lengthens muscle fibers and connective tissues such as tendons and fascia.
⚙ For students: stretching releases tension from long hours of sitting, studying, or using devices.
★ Range of motion (ROM): how far a joint can move in a given direction, such as how far you can raise your arm or bend your knee.
⚙ Flexibility training keeps joints lubricated, aligned, and functional.
⚙ For non-athletes: better ROM means fewer aches, easier movement, and less risk of injury during daily tasks.
★ Static stretching: holding a stretch.
★ Dynamic stretching: moving through a range of motion.
★ Reducing stiffness: stiffness can come from inactivity, stress, or repetitive movements. Flexibility training loosens tight areas, improves circulation, and promotes relaxation.
⚙ For students: less neck and back pain, better posture, and more comfort during long study sessions.
Difference: static stretching holds one position still. Dynamic stretching keeps moving through the range, like arm circles or leg swings.

WHY FLEXIBILITY TRAINING MATTERS TO STUDENTS
⚙ Postural health: reduces slouching and spinal strain from prolonged sitting.
⚙ Injury prevention: keeps muscles and joints supple, reducing the risk of strains or sprains.
⚙ Stress relief: promotes mindfulness and relaxation through slow, controlled movement.
⚙ Movement efficiency: enhances coordination and fluidity in daily tasks.
⚙ Accessibility: needs no equipment and can be done anywhere: home, dorm, or classroom.

FLEXIBILITY MMFIT GUIDELINES
⚙ Motivation: recovery, relaxation, and injury prevention.
⚙ Mode: flexibility routines like yoga or stretching.
⚙ Intensity: light to moderate (RPE 2 to 5).
⚙ Frequency: daily or post-workout.
⚙ Time: 10 to 20 minutes per session.

COMPARING THE THREE MMFIT GUIDELINES
⚙ Aerobic: RPE 4 to 8, 3 to 5 times a week, 20 to 60 minutes.
⚙ Strength: RPE 5 to 8, 2 to 3 times a week, 20 to 45 minutes.
⚙ Flexibility: RPE 2 to 5, daily or post-workout, 10 to 20 minutes.
Difference: aerobic training has the longest sessions (up to 60 minutes), strength training is the least frequent (2 to 3 times a week), and flexibility training is the lightest and can be done daily.

EXAMPLE: A WEEKLY ROUTINE WITH MMFIT
Added example: a student who wants less stress and better posture.
⚙ Monday, Wednesday, Friday: 30 minutes of brisk walking or dancing at RPE 5 (aerobic).
⚙ Tuesday and Thursday: 30 minutes of push-ups, squats, and wall sits at RPE 6 to 7 (strength).
⚙ Every day after studying or exercise: 10 to 15 minutes of stretching at RPE 3 (flexibility).
★ Each part stays inside its MMFIT guideline for frequency, intensity, and time.

COMMON MISTAKES
⚙ Thinking RPE comes from a heart rate monitor. It is based on your personal perception.
⚙ Mixing up the frequencies: aerobic is 3 to 5 times a week, strength is 2 to 3, flexibility is daily or post-workout.
⚙ Thinking strength training is only for building big muscles. Even light resistance improves tone, posture, and endurance.
⚙ Forgetting that gravity (wall sits, stair climbing) counts as resistance.
⚙ Placing Tabata in the maximal RPE zone. The module puts circuit training and Tabata at RPE 7 to 8 (hard); sprinting and intense HIIT are 9 to 10.

EXAM TIPS
⚙ Know that "aerobic" means "with oxygen".
⚙ Memorize the MMFIT guideline for each training type, especially the RPE range, frequency, and time.
⚙ Memorize the RPE scale with one example activity per level.
⚙ Know the four key concepts of strength training: uses resistance, builds muscle, improves bone density, enhances functional strength.
⚙ Know the three key concepts of flexibility training: stretching muscles, improving joint range of motion, reducing stiffness.
