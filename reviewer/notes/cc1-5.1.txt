5.0-5.1 Introduction and Understanding a Project
Projects vs operations, project features, organizational structures, project managers, and PMOs

MODULE OVERVIEW AND OBJECTIVES
Project management is a vital skill at work today. Successful project execution often decides whether an organization succeeds.
From technology to creative fields, project management gives a structured way to reach specific goals within defined limits (constraints).
This module covers project management principles, methodologies, and practices for planning, executing, and delivering projects.
You will learn the core concepts, the project life cycle, and various project management methodologies, so you can manage both personal and professional projects.
⚙ Objective 1: Define what makes something a project and identify the key elements of project management.
⚙ Objective 2: Explain the stages of the project life cycle and why they matter for successful projects.
⚙ Objective 3: Compare project management methodologies and choose the right method for a specific type of project.

KEY TERMS
★ Project: a temporary endeavor with a unique purpose. It is carried out to reach specific goals and produce unique outcomes, often something new, improved, or customized.
★ Endeavor: a serious effort to do something.
★ Operations: regular work that is ongoing and repetitive, aimed at keeping the organization stable.
★ Business as usual (BAU): the video's name for operations, meaning all the everyday, ongoing, normal activities that keep an organization running.
★ Constraints: limits a project must work within, such as resources (time, money, materials), scope, and quality standards.
★ Scope: what needs to be achieved, meaning what is and is not included in the project.
★ Deliverable: the final product, service, or result a project hands over.
★ Stakeholders: the people and groups who are affected by or interested in the project.
★ Progressive elaboration: refining a project's original goals and plans step by step as new information appears.
EASIER: A project is like planning a wedding: it has a start date, an end date, and one unique result. Operations are like cooking dinner every night: the same job, again and again, with no end.

CONCEPT OF A PROJECT
Projects are at the heart of business and technology innovation, such as developing a new product, launching a marketing campaign, or setting up a government service.
★ Projects are temporary efforts to create unique products, services, or results.
They exist across industries, from software development to building infrastructure, and help organizations adapt, grow, and succeed.

KEY CHARACTERISTICS OF PROJECTS
⚙ Temporary: projects are not permanent. They have a defined beginning and end.
⚙ Unique output: each project gives a unique result, whether a product, service, or process.
⚙ Goal-oriented: projects are driven by specific objectives, which set the project's direction and purpose.
Difference: operations are repetitive and aim to keep the organization stable. Projects are aimed at change and innovation.
Difference: operations are ongoing. Projects are finite, with a clear start and end.

FEATURES OF A PROJECT
Every project, in any industry or size, has these features. They explain why projects need special management approaches.
⚙ Temporary: projects are time-bound, with a specific start and end. This time limit defines what can be achieved (the scope).
⚙ Unique output: projects create unique outcomes, and no two projects are identical. Even with similar processes, each project is adapted to its own goals.
⚙ Progressive elaboration: as the project moves forward, new information may come up, refining the original goals and plans.
⚙ Constraints: projects face limits such as resources (time, money, materials), scope (what needs to be achieved), and quality standards.
⚙ Resource and quality requirements: projects need careful use of resources so the final deliverable meets specific quality expectations.
EASIER: Progressive elaboration is like zooming in on a map. At the start you see the general route; as you travel, you see more details and adjust.

WHAT A PROJECT IS NOT (FROM THE VIDEO)
★ Business as usual is not a project. Examples: answering the phone, making coffee, replying to emails, writing reports for your boss, and regular weekly meetings.
In a company, people work in functional units (or, in government, departments), and their work has a narrow focus. Sales staff work to increase sales; IT staff maintain the IT systems.
Examples of projects from the video: developing a new smartphone, designing and running a marketing campaign, improving business-as-usual processes, a wedding, and moving into a new house.

PROJECT CHARACTERISTICS FROM THE VIDEO
⚙ Temporary: every project has a start date and an end date. Even late projects stop at some point. If the work never stops, it is business as usual, not a project.
⚙ Brings change: projects create outputs that business as usual uses to change. Example outputs: a new product (which may need new support and sales processes), or a more efficient process, such as moving from paper to an automated system.
⚙ Unique: even similar projects always produce different results.
⚙ Cross-functional: projects usually involve several departments, and in large organizations they are often global. Rolling out a new email system worldwide is far more complex than in one small department in one country.
⚙ Risky: risk means uncertainty, the things that might go wrong. Example: what if nobody buys the new phone? Projects are always more risky than business as usual because you often do things for the first time.

HOW TO TELL A PROJECT FROM OPERATIONS
⚙ Step 1: Ask whether the work has a defined start and end. No end means it is operations (business as usual).
⚙ Step 2: Ask whether it produces a unique result. Repeating the same result means operations.
⚙ Step 3: Ask whether it is driven by a specific goal that brings change. Keeping things running as they are means operations.
⚙ Step 4: If the answers are yes, yes, and yes, it is a project.

EXAMPLE: CLASSIFY FOUR ACTIVITIES
Added example:
⚙ Replying to customer emails every day: no end, same result each day. Operations.
⚙ Building a new mobile banking app by December: has an end, unique result, specific goal. Project.
⚙ Running payroll every month: repeats forever. Operations.
⚙ Moving the office to a new building: has an end, unique result, brings change. Project.

EXAMPLES OF PRACTICAL PROJECTS (KOREA)
★ E-government project: its purpose is to establish public informatization (using IT in public services) through a framework that delivers e-government services to the public efficiently.
⚙ Application: 756 public and private informatization projects in total, including the Korean Statistical Information Service (KOSIS) and the Corporate Competitiveness Support System (as of March 2018).
⚙ Practical examples: customer-specific administrative information sharing system, resident service integrated system, urban railway system, 911 reporting service expansion, and a health insurance fee portal.
★ Smart factory project: its purpose is to establish factory automation and digitalization using IT systems such as IoT, MES, PLM, and SCM, to make manufacturers more competitive.
⚙ Application: 5,003 smart factories were built by the end of 2017, with a goal of 20,000 by 2022.
⚙ Practical examples: a customized MES system; MES/POP for inventory, process management, and lowering defect rates; a customized ERP/web control system for special manufacturing processes; and real-time facility monitoring.
★ Next Generation Finance project: its purpose is to build an ideal integrated system of business and IT that reflects and supports the enterprise-wide strategies of financial institutions.
⚙ Application: the system has been built, or is being built, in most commercial banks and local banks in Korea since 2000.
⚙ Practical examples: next-generation systems of Shinhan Bank, Hana Bank, Daegu Bank, JB Jeonbuk Bank, and Kyungnam Bank; to be developed by KB Kookmin Bank, Bank of Korea, Korea Postbank, Hanwha Life Insurance, and others.
Added example, what the abbreviations mean:
IoT (Internet of Things): everyday devices and machines connected to the internet so they can send data.
MES (Manufacturing Execution System): software that tracks and controls work on the factory floor.
PLM (Product Lifecycle Management): software that manages a product's data from design to retirement.
SCM (Supply Chain Management): managing the flow of materials and goods from suppliers to customers.
ERP (Enterprise Resource Planning): one system that connects a company's main business processes, like finance, inventory, and HR.
POP (Point of Production): collecting data directly where products are made.

PROJECT ORGANIZATIONAL STRUCTURES
Projects are run inside organizational structures that differ in how much authority the project manager has and how flexibly resources can be assigned.
Choosing the right structure is critical: it decides how efficiently resources are used and how well the project is controlled.
★ The three primary types are the functional organization, the matrix organization, and the projectized organization.

FUNCTIONAL ORGANIZATION
★ Structure: departments are organized by functions (for example Marketing, IT, Finance).
★ Project role: project work is given to functional departments, and project managers often have limited authority.
⚙ Advantage: high efficiency within specialized departments.
⚙ Disadvantages: limited collaboration across departments and slower decision-making.

MATRIX ORGANIZATION
★ Structure: combines functional and projectized structures. Team members report to both a functional manager and a project manager.
⚙ Weak matrix: low authority for project managers.
⚙ Balanced matrix: shared authority.
⚙ Strong matrix: high authority for project managers.
⚙ Advantage: encourages resource sharing and collaboration.
⚙ Disadvantage: dual reporting (two bosses) can create conflicts.
EASIER: In a matrix, you have two bosses: your department head and the project manager. Weak, balanced, and strong describe how much power the project manager has compared with the department head.

PROJECTIZED ORGANIZATION
★ Structure: the whole organization is built around projects. Project managers have full authority over resources and decisions.
⚙ Advantages: strong focus on project goals and fast decision-making.
⚙ Disadvantage: limited stability for employees once the project ends.
★ Project manager authority from lowest to highest: functional, weak matrix, balanced matrix, strong matrix, projectized.
★ The right structure depends on the organization's goals, the complexity of the project, and resource availability.

EXAMPLE: IDENTIFY THE STRUCTURE
Added example:
⚙ A programmer reports to the IT head and also to a project manager, and both share decisions equally. Two bosses means matrix; shared authority means balanced matrix.
⚙ A company's teams are each formed for one client project and disband when it ends, and the project manager controls the budget. This is projectized; its risk is that staff have less job stability after the project.
⚙ A project is handed to the Finance department, and the project manager must ask the Finance head for every resource. This is functional, with limited project manager authority.

RESPONSIBILITIES AND ROLES OF A PROJECT MANAGER
★ Project manager: the person responsible for making sure project goals are met within the set constraints.
The project manager drives the project team and plays a key role in aligning resources, managing risks, and communicating with stakeholders.
★ Five core responsibilities: scope, schedule, cost, risk, and quality management.
⚙ Scope management: define and control what is included in the project.
⚙ Schedule management: set up and manage the project timeline.
⚙ Cost management: oversee the project budget and control expenses.
⚙ Risk management: identify, assess, and reduce (mitigate) possible risks.
⚙ Quality management: make sure deliverables meet the required standards.
★ Four key skills: problem-solving, communication, negotiation, and leadership.
⚙ Problem-solving: deal with project challenges effectively.
⚙ Communication: keep communication clear with the team and stakeholders.
⚙ Negotiation: resolve conflicts and secure project resources.
⚙ Leadership: inspire and guide the team toward success.
Project managers balance the project's constraints while keeping stakeholders informed and the project on track.
Difference: schedule management is about TIME (the timeline). Cost management is about MONEY (the budget). Scope management is about WHAT is included.

PROJECT MANAGEMENT OFFICE (PMO)
★ PMO (Project Management Office): an organizational unit that centralizes and standardizes the project management process.
★ The PMO's role is to provide governance, set best practices, and make sure projects match strategic objectives.
★ Governance: setting the rules and standards for how projects are run and checked.
Types of PMO, from least to most authority:
⚙ Supportive PMO: provides templates, training, and best practices.
⚙ Controlling PMO: enforces compliance with standards and methodologies.
⚙ Directive PMO: directly manages projects, with high authority over project teams.
Roles of a PMO:
⚙ Governance: establishes project management standards.
⚙ Resource allocation: manages resources across multiple projects.
⚙ Risk management: provides risk identification and mitigation support.
⚙ Performance tracking: monitors project progress and alignment with strategic goals.
PMOs make sure projects support organizational goals and resources are used well across the portfolio (the whole set) of projects.
EASIER: Supportive PMO is a coach (gives tools). Controlling PMO is a referee (checks rules). Directive PMO is the boss (runs projects).
Difference: a project manager runs ONE project. A PMO oversees standards and resources across MANY projects.

COMMON MISTAKES
Calling routine work a project. Daily emails or weekly meetings have no end date, so they are operations.
Mixing up the matrix types. Weak means LOW project manager authority, strong means HIGH.
Mixing up PMO types. Supportive gives tools, controlling enforces rules, directive manages projects.
Saying functional organizations give project managers full authority. That is projectized.
Forgetting that the projectized disadvantage is job stability after the project ends.
