FAILURES IN DESIGN
★ HCI is a complex problem solving process that looks into creating and designing interactive products and services.
There is a lot of focus on how the users will interact with the product or service.
HCI looks into ideas like planned usage, target area, resources, cost, and viability.
The lecture shows ten real world design fails as pictures only, with no written explanation on the slides.
Fail 1: toilet stalls whose doors leave a large gap, so the toilets are exposed (a privacy problem).
Fail 2: a store window sale sign whose letter panels read SASA LELE across the rows instead of SALE, with SALE 50% banners below.
Fail 3: a pair of glass doors with pull style handles that are labeled PUSH.
Fail 4: a jeepney sign reading MINIMUM FEAR P11.00 (a typo for fare).
Fail 5: a bread package labeled English French Toast with a Spanish flag, the word American, a pizza picture, and German style text; the caption jokes about offending many countries at once.
Fail 6: a meme about building an orphanage's website without a Home page.
Fail 7: a zoo style sign with a hippo silhouette icon and the label Hipopótamos, shown with two outline redrawings of the icon.
Fail 8: food tray labels reading Chicken Adobo 45 and Adobong Manok P45 (the same dish named in two languages).
Fail 9: a framed sign reading Life is short lick the bowl placed on a toilet tank, with a caption where Mom says to leave the sign in the kitchen where it belongs.
Fail 10: a shirt printed with THINGER STRANGS, a misspelled imitation of the Stranger Things logo.

DONALD NORMAN AND THE NORMAN DOOR
★ Donald A. Norman is a cognitive psychologist, author, and lecturer.
★ Norman is dubbed the Father of User Experience Design because of his contributions to interaction design and user interface and user experience (UI/UX).
★ Norman door: basically any door that is confusing or difficult to use.
⚙ Test for a Norman door: determine whether or not the door makes sense as it is being approached.
EASIER: If you cannot tell how to use a door just by walking up to it, it is a Norman door.
The slides illustrate this with a glass door that has several long handles, and a picture of one hand pushing a flat panel while another hand pulls a handle.
★ As the solution to the Norman door, Norman formulated six principles of interaction design based on the concept of Human Centered Design.

THE SIX PRINCIPLES OF INTERACTION DESIGN
⚙ The six principles are Affordances, Signifiers, Constraints, Mappings, Feedback, and Conceptual Models.
Discoverability is a later addition and is not one of the original six.

AFFORDANCES
★ Affordances: in this context, to afford means to give a clue.
★ Affordances focus on the qualities or properties of an object that define its possible uses or make clear how it can or should be used.
Examples shown: a red mug with a handle and a computer mouse with a scroll wheel.
EASIER: An affordance is what the object itself hints that you can do with it.

SIGNIFIERS
★ Signifiers are indicators of any type that communicate the action needed so the affordance can take place.
A signifier can be any visual mark, sound, or perceivable indicator that communicates appropriate behavior to a person, such as a cursor for most digital user interfaces.
Examples shown: a text field with a cursor in a form with Title, Price, Location (optional), and Description fields, and enabled versus disabled (greyed out) buttons.
EASIER: The affordance is what can be done, and the signifier is the signal that shows how to do it.

CONSTRAINTS
★ Constraints are limitations or restrictions that give clues that allow us to determine a course of action by limiting the possible actions available to the user.
Constraints limit the set of possible actions that can be done.
⚙ There are four kinds of constraints: Physical, Cultural, Semantic, and Logical.
★ Physical constraint: shown with a lock cylinder and key.
★ Cultural constraint: shown with chopsticks, a fork and spoon, a fork spoon and knife set, and a chopsticks and spoon set.
★ Semantic constraint: shown with a car windshield carrying a sticker (the slide gives no further explanation).
★ Logical constraint: shown with a shape sorter cube toy with colored blocks.

MAPPINGS
★ Mappings look into the relationship between a set of inputs and a set of permissible outputs.
⚙ The relationship between controls and their actions follows the principles of good mapping, enhanced as much as possible through spatial layout and temporal contiguity.
Example shown: a New Customer phone form with a numeric keypad (digits with letters such as ABC and DEF).

FEEDBACK
★ Feedback happens when an object or system notifies the user that it is working towards the completion of its objective.
Feedbacks communicate the results of an action.
Example shown: a Loading... Please Wait screen with a progress bar.

CONCEPTUAL MODELS
★ A conceptual model is an explanation, usually highly simplified, of how something works.
There are often multiple conceptual models of a product or device, especially complex systems.
Example shown: a labeled scissors diagram with inner blade, cutting edge, back, blade, point or tip, screw, pivot point, handle, finger rest, stopper or silencer, and finger ring.

DISCOVERABILITY
★ Discoverability, also known as visibility, looks into the degree of ease with which the user can find all the elements and features of a new system when they first encounter it.
Discoverability was not part of the original six principles but was added in later years due to its relevance.
Example shown: a TV remote labeled 43 baffling buttons.
