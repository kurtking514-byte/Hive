Module 1 Fundamental Movement Skills
The three skill groups: locomotor, non-locomotor, and manipulative

LEARNING OUTCOMES
By the end of this module, the student should be able to do three things.
Outcome one: discuss the three skill groups of the fundamental movement skills.
Outcome two: demonstrate effectively the fundamental movement skills.
Outcome three: appreciate the importance of the fundamental skills through properly performing the movement skills.

ACTIVATING PRIOR KNOWLEDGE
Your teacher may start the lesson with three questions. Try to answer them first, then check the answers below.
Q: What does it mean when we say fundamental? What are fundamental movement skills, then?
★ Fundamental means basic. It is the foundation that other things are built on.
★ Fundamental movement skills are the basic movements of the body that form the foundation for more complex skills in sports, dance, and daily activities.
Q: What are its three skill groups?
★ The three skill groups are locomotor, non-locomotor, and manipulative.
Q: Demonstrate one movement under each skill group.
★ Sample answer: walking (locomotor), bending (non-locomotor), and throwing (manipulative).
EASIER: Think of the alphabet. You must learn the letters first before you can read words. Fundamental movement skills are the letters of movement, and sports are the words and sentences.

THE THREE SKILL GROUPS
★ Locomotor skills are movements done by moving from one place to another.
★ Non-locomotor skills are movements done while staying in one place (stationary).
★ Manipulative skills are movements that include objects to perform a skill.
EASIER: Locomotor means you travel. Your body goes from Point A to Point B, like walking to school.
EASIER: Non-locomotor means you stay. Your feet remain in the same spot, but the body part moves, like bending to tie your shoelace.
EASIER: Manipulative means you handle an object. To manipulate means to handle something, like a ball, with your hands or feet.
EASIER: Memory trick: Travel, Stay, Handle.
EASIER: A quick test. Ask yourself two questions. Is an object used? If yes, it is manipulative. If no object is used, does the body travel to a new place? If yes, it is locomotor. If no, it is non-locomotor.

LOCOMOTOR MOVEMENT SKILLS
★ There are 11 locomotor movement skills: walking, running, jogging, crawling, creeping, hopping, skipping, shuffling, leaping, galloping, and jumping and landing.
EASIER: Locomotion means moving from place to place. Every skill in this list takes the body somewhere.

WALKING
★ Walking is the transfer of weight from one foot to the other while moving forward or backward.
★ In walking, one foot is always in contact with the ground, and the arms swing freely in opposite directions.
EASIER: When you walk, you never fly. There is always at least one foot on the floor.
EASIER: The arms swing opposite to each other. When the right arm swings forward, the left arm swings back.

RUNNING
★ Running is done by propelling oneself rapidly on foot.
EASIER: To propel means to push forward with force. Running is fast walking pushed to the next level.
Added example: In running, there is a moment when both feet are off the ground at the same time. In walking, this never happens.

JOGGING
★ Jogging is faster than walking and slower than running.
EASIER: Think of a speed ladder from slowest to fastest: walking, jogging, running. Jogging is the middle step.

CRAWLING
★ Crawling is being low to the ground and doing a sliding movement, without lifting the belly from the ground.
EASIER: Picture a soldier crawling under barbed wire. The belly stays on the floor the whole time. This is also called the commando crawl.

CREEPING
★ Creeping is done on all-fours (hands and feet), either on your knees or on your toes, while moving around.
EASIER: Picture a baby moving on hands and knees. The belly is lifted off the floor.
EASIER: Everyday speech may call this crawling, but in this module crawling means belly on the ground, and creeping means all-fours with the belly lifted.

HOPPING
★ Hopping is a springing action that involves taking off from one foot and landing on that same foot.
EASIER: Think of playing hopscotch on one foot. You leave the ground with one foot and come down on the same foot.

SKIPPING
★ Skipping is a combination of a step and a hop (step-hop pattern), first on one foot and then on the other.
⚙ Guide procedure for skipping:
⚙ Step 1: Step one foot forward and lift the other foot from the ground.
⚙ Step 2: Hop on the foot that is on the ground.
⚙ Step 3: Then, do the same on the other foot.
EASIER: Say it as a rhythm: step-hop, step-hop. Each foot takes a turn.

SHUFFLING
★ Shuffling is similar to skipping but with a draw backwards instead of a hop.
⚙ Guide procedure for shuffling:
⚙ Step 1: Step one foot forward and lift the other foot from the ground.
⚙ Step 2: Drag the foot that is on the floor backwards, covering a little distance.
⚙ Step 3: Then, do the same on the other foot.
EASIER: Shuffling is a low, sliding version of skipping. Instead of springing up in a hop, the foot drags along the floor for a short distance.

GALLOPING
★ Galloping is not a hop or a skip, and it should never become quick enough for the movement to become a run. This movement mimics a horse's gallop.
★ In galloping, one foot leads while the toes of the back foot chase the heel of the lead foot.
★ In galloping, both feet leave the ground and land on the back foot, followed by the lead foot.
★ While galloping, the knees are slightly bent.
EASIER: Imagine riding a pretend horse. The same foot always stays in front, and the back foot keeps trying to catch up to it.
EASIER: Mimics means copies. You are copying the rhythm of a horse.

LEAPING
★ Leaping is characterized by a take-off on one foot, a long flight phase, and a landing on the opposite foot.
★ In leaping, the strides should be long and cover a farther distance.
EASIER: Think of jumping over a small puddle. You push off one foot, stay in the air for a long moment, and land on the other foot.
EASIER: Flight phase means the time that you are in the air.

JUMPING AND LANDING
★ Jumping and landing is a movement that involves taking off from one or both feet and landing on both feet.
★ In jumping and landing, the base of support (the distance of the feet from each other) should be shoulder-width.
EASIER: Base of support means the area your feet cover on the floor. A shoulder-width stance keeps you steady, like a table with legs spread properly.
Added example: A safe landing also bends the knees a little to absorb the force, like a spring.

NON-LOCOMOTOR MOVEMENT SKILLS
★ There are 7 non-locomotor movement skills: bending, stretching or extending, lifting or raising, twisting, turning, swinging, and swaying.
EASIER: In all seven, the body stays in place. The arms, legs, trunk, or whole body can move, but you do not travel.

BENDING
★ Bending is a movement around any joint to move from a straight position into a curved position.
EASIER: A joint is where two bones meet, like the elbow, knee, or waist. Bending your elbow turns a straight arm into a curve.

STRETCHING OR EXTENDING
★ Stretching or extending is done by straightening or extending any part of the body from the joints.
EASIER: This is the opposite of bending. A curved arm becomes straight again.

LIFTING OR RAISING
★ Lifting or raising is done by elevating a part of the body, usually the arms and legs.
EASIER: Raising your hand in class or lifting a leg to the side are examples.

TWISTING
★ Twisting is rotating a body part while the base stays stationary.
EASIER: Your feet stay planted while the upper body rotates, like wringing a towel.

TURNING
★ Turning is done by a circular movement of the body, including the base.
EASIER: In turning, the feet move too. You spin your whole body, like turning around to face the back of the room.
EASIER: Twisting versus turning: in twisting the base (feet) stays still, and in turning the base moves with the body.

SWINGING
★ Swinging is moving a body part side to side or forward and back, like a pendulum.
EASIER: Think of the pendulum of a wall clock or an arm swinging back and forth.

SWAYING
★ Swaying is shifting weight from one side to the other side smoothly.
EASIER: Think of a tree moving in the wind, or rocking gently from the left foot to the right foot during a slow song.
EASIER: Swinging versus swaying: swinging moves a body part like a pendulum, while swaying shifts the weight of the body smoothly from side to side.

MANIPULATIVE MOVEMENT SKILLS
★ Manipulative movement skills are grouped into three: sending or propelling objects, receiving and controlling objects, and continuous control skills.

SENDING OR PROPELLING OBJECTS
★ The sending or propelling skills are throwing, kicking, striking or hitting, and volleying.
EASIER: Sending means the object leaves you and goes somewhere else.
Added example: Throwing is sending an object through the air using the hand or hands, such as a softball throw.
Added example: Kicking is sending an object by hitting it with the foot, such as kicking a soccer ball.
Added example: Striking or hitting is sending an object by hitting it with a body part or an implement, such as a bat, racket, or paddle.
Added example: Volleying is hitting an object while it is in the air without letting it rest, such as a volleyball set or a badminton shuttlecock hit.

RECEIVING AND CONTROLLING OBJECTS
★ The receiving and controlling skills are catching and trapping.
EASIER: Receiving means the object comes to you and you gain control of it.
Added example: Catching is receiving an object, usually with the hands, and gaining control of it.
Added example: Trapping is stopping and controlling an object, usually a ball, with a body part such as the foot or chest.

CONTINUOUS CONTROL SKILLS
★ The continuous control skills are dribbling (hands or feet) and rolling.
EASIER: Continuous means you keep controlling the object again and again, not just once.
Added example: Dribbling with the hands is repeated bouncing of a ball, as in basketball. Dribbling with the feet is repeated light kicks that keep the ball close, as in soccer.
Added example: Rolling is moving an object along the ground using the hand or hands, such as rolling a ball in bowling.

EXAMPLE: SKIPPING WITH THE RIGHT FOOT FIRST
⚙ Start standing tall with both feet on the ground.
⚙ Step 1: Step the right foot forward and lift the left foot from the ground.
⚙ Step 2: Hop on the right foot, which is the foot on the ground.
⚙ Step 3: Do the same on the other foot. Step the left foot forward, lift the right foot, and hop on the left foot.
⚙ Final result: The pattern is step-hop on the right, then step-hop on the left. Each foot gets one step and one hop.

EXAMPLE: SHUFFLING WITH THE RIGHT FOOT FIRST
⚙ Start standing tall with both feet on the ground.
⚙ Step 1: Step the right foot forward and lift the left foot from the ground.
⚙ Step 2: Drag the right foot, which is on the floor, backwards, covering a little distance.
⚙ Step 3: Do the same on the other foot. Step the left foot forward, lift the right foot, and drag the left foot backwards a little.
⚙ Final result: The pattern is step-drag on the right, then step-drag on the left. There is no hop, and the feet stay low.

EXAMPLE: GALLOPING WITH THE RIGHT FOOT LEADING
⚙ Start with the knees slightly bent.
⚙ Step 1: Step forward with the right foot. The right foot is now the lead foot.
⚙ Step 2: Bring the left foot (the back foot) up so that its toes chase the heel of the right foot.
⚙ Step 3: Both feet leave the ground.
⚙ Step 4: Land on the back (left) foot first, followed by the lead (right) foot.
⚙ Final result: The right foot stays in front the whole time, the knees stay slightly bent, and the speed stays slow enough that it never becomes a run.

EXAMPLE: CLASSIFYING MOVEMENTS INTO SKILL GROUPS
⚙ Activity: Bouncing a basketball in one spot. An object is used, so it is manipulative (dribbling, a continuous control skill), even though the body stays in place.
⚙ Activity: Bending forward at the waist to touch your toes. No object is used and the body does not travel, so it is non-locomotor (bending).
⚙ Activity: Moving across the room with a step-hop on alternate feet. The body travels, so it is locomotor (skipping).
⚙ Activity: Stopping a rolling soccer ball with your foot. An object is used and it is being received, so it is manipulative (trapping, a receiving and controlling skill).
⚙ Activity: Rocking gently from the left foot to the right foot without moving from your spot. No object, no travel, so it is non-locomotor (swaying).

EXAMPLE: CHOOSING THE RIGHT LOCOMOTOR SKILL
⚙ Take off from one foot and land on the same foot: hopping.
⚙ Take off from one foot, stay in the air a long time, and land on the opposite foot: leaping.
⚙ Take off from one or both feet and land on both feet with feet shoulder-width apart: jumping and landing.
⚙ Step and hop on one foot, then step and hop on the other: skipping.
⚙ Step and drag the foot backwards on the floor, then repeat on the other foot: shuffling.
⚙ Same foot always leads, both feet leave the ground, and the back foot lands first: galloping.
⚙ Belly stays on the ground and the body slides: crawling.
⚙ On hands and knees (or hands and toes) with the belly lifted: creeping.

EXAMPLE: DEMONSTRATING ONE MOVEMENT FROM EACH SKILL GROUP
⚙ Locomotor example, walking: Step 1: Stand tall. Step 2: Transfer your weight to one foot and step forward with the other. Step 3: Swing the arms freely in opposite directions. Result: one foot is always touching the ground.
⚙ Non-locomotor example, bending: Step 1: Stand straight. Step 2: Bend the elbow so the arm goes from straight to curved. Step 3: Stretch it back to straight. Result: you bent and stretched without moving from your place.
⚙ Manipulative example, throwing: Step 1: Hold a ball in your hand. Step 2: Bring the arm back. Step 3: Release the ball forward through the air. Result: you sent an object away from your body.

COMMON MISTAKES
Mistake: Saying that hopping and leaping are the same. Hopping lands on the same foot as the take-off foot. Leaping lands on the opposite foot after a long flight phase.
Mistake: Saying that skipping and galloping are the same. Skipping is a step-hop on alternate feet. Galloping is not a hop or a skip, and one foot stays in the lead.
Mistake: Saying that shuffling includes a hop. Shuffling has a backward draw (drag) instead of a hop.
Mistake: Saying that crawling and creeping are the same. In crawling, the belly stays on the ground. In creeping, the body is on all-fours.
Mistake: Saying that twisting and turning are the same. Twisting keeps the base stationary. Turning moves the whole body including the base.
Mistake: Saying that swinging and swaying are the same. Swinging is pendulum-like movement of a body part. Swaying is smooth shifting of weight from side to side.
Mistake: Calling a skill non-locomotor just because the body stays in place while using an object. If an object is used, the skill is manipulative.
Mistake: Letting galloping speed up into running. Galloping should never become quick enough to be a run.
Mistake: Thinking that walking has a moment when both feet are in the air. In walking, one foot is always in contact with the ground.

EXAM TIPS
Memorize the three groups with the trick Travel, Stay, Handle.
Memorize the counts: 11 locomotor skills and 7 non-locomotor skills.
Memorize the three manipulative subgroups: sending or propelling, receiving and controlling, and continuous control.
Compare similar pairs side by side: hopping versus leaping, crawling versus creeping, twisting versus turning, swinging versus swaying, skipping versus shuffling versus galloping.
Remember the key numbers and positions: base of support is shoulder-width, and the knees are slightly bent in galloping.

★ Eleven.

★ A step and a hop (step-hop pattern), first on one foot and then on the other.

★ A draw (drag) backwards.
