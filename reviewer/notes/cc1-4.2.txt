4.2 Analog Data and Digital Data
Continuous vs discrete data, signals, noise, and digitizing

KEY TERMS
★ Analog data: data that is continuous and can represent an infinite range of values within a given range.
★ Digital data: data that is discrete, represented by a series of separate values (often 0s and 1s in binary form).
★ Continuous: changing smoothly with no gaps or jumps. Between any two values there is always another value.
★ Discrete: made of separate, fixed values or steps, with nothing in between.
★ Analog signal: a signal that is continuously variable (continuously changing), like the time shown by the hands of an analog clock.
★ Digital signal: a signal that can only take fixed levels or symbols, such as 0 and 1.
★ Noise: unwanted interference that gets added to a signal and degrades its quality.
EASIER: Analog is a ramp: you can stand at any height. Digital is a staircase: you can only stand on a step.
EASIER: Analog is like a dimmer knob that can be at any brightness. Digital is like a light switch that is only on or off.

ANALOG DATA
Analog signals are not restricted to discrete steps. They vary smoothly, following the continuous flow of information.
★ Continuity: analog signals change smoothly over time.
★ Infinite range: analog data can take any value within its range, which makes it ideal for representing natural signals.
★ Susceptible to noise: because analog signals vary continuously, they easily pick up interference (noise), which can degrade quality over long distances.
Examples of analog data:
⚙ Sound waves: a person's voice, which has varying pitch and volume, is an analog signal.
⚙ Light intensity: the brightness of natural light varies smoothly and continuously.
⚙ Temperature changes: temperature changes continuously over time and does not change in discrete steps.
Analog data can be observed in:
⚙ Analog clocks: time moves continuously with the movement of the clock hands.
⚙ Old vinyl records: sound is recorded as a continuous wave form on the vinyl.

THE ANALOG CLOCK IDEA
The face of an analog clock only has 12 numbers, but you can always tell how many minutes have passed by how far the minute hand is between two numbers.
As the hands sweep across the face, the time shown is updated continuously with nearly infinite precision.
★ The key idea of analog is continuous change.

ANALOG SIGNALS IN ELECTRONICS
★ AC (alternating current) voltage in a house is an analog signal. It changes continuously at a rate of 50 to 60 hertz (Hz), depending on the country.
If you could freeze time and look at the voltage at different moments, you would see that its value is different at every moment.
Analog is not only found in wall sockets or AC. AC is just a clear example.
★ Accelerometer: a sensor that gives an analog output. Its output voltage changes depending on how much acceleration is happening.
★ To use an analog voltage in a digital system such as an Arduino (a small programmable circuit board), the signal must first be converted to digital.
Other examples of the analog-to-digital change: old cell phones used to be analog and are now all digital, and most countries have changed (or are changing) their television transmissions from analog to digital.

DIGITAL DATA
Digital data is preferred for computers because it matches the binary system and can be stored, processed, and transmitted with fewer errors and greater stability.
★ Discrete values: digital data is made of separate values, not a continuous range.
★ Finite set of values: digital signals are usually restricted to specific steps or levels (for example, 0 and 1 in binary).
★ Less prone to noise: digital data is less affected by degradation over distance and can be easily regenerated, which makes it ideal for reliable storage and communication.
EASIER: Regenerated means the receiver can rebuild the clean signal. A slightly blurry 1 is still clearly a 1, so it can be redrawn as a perfect 1 and sent on.
Examples of digital data:
⚙ Binary code: digital systems represent data using bits (0s and 1s), forming a series of discrete values.
⚙ Digital images: images are stored as pixels, each with a specific color value, making a digital representation of a scene.
⚙ Digital audio: sound is stored as discrete samples, such as the pulse-code modulation (PCM) used in CDs.
★ PCM (pulse-code modulation): a way to store sound digitally by measuring (sampling) the wave at regular intervals and saving each measurement as a number.
Digital data can be observed in:
⚙ Digital clocks: time is shown in discrete numbers for hours, minutes, and seconds.
⚙ MP3 music files: audio is stored digitally, using specific bits to represent sound information.

THE DIGITAL CLOCK IDEA
A digital clock can only show the numbers 0 to 9 for each digit of the time.
Even if you zoom in very close, you cannot see how close the clock is to flipping to the next digit.
★ In digital, the values are fixed to certain digits or levels.

DIGITAL SIGNALS IN ELECTRONICS
In digital electronics, signals are represented with fixed symbols like 0 and 1. Those 0s and 1s are made from voltage.
★ A digital signal has a low level and a high level. Low voltage means a digital 0. High voltage means a digital 1.
⚙ Simple example: 0 volts is a digital 0, and a high voltage such as 5 volts is a digital 1.
Real digital is not that exact. Depending on the transistor technology, there is a range (band) of voltages that counts as 0 and a range that counts as 1.
⚙ The video's example bands for an Arduino Uno: digital 0 is 0 V up to 0.5 V, and digital 1 is 3.5 V up to 5 V.
★ Undefined region: the voltage zone between the low band and the high band. A signal in this zone could be read as either 0 or 1, and you cannot always predict which.
Digital logic must give every signal a value, so a voltage in the undefined region still gets turned into a 0 or a 1, just not reliably.
★ Key difference: analog voltages change continuously, while digital voltages have defined levels.
★ Digital signals are really analog signals whose voltage levels have been given special meanings (0 or 1).

HOW TO READ A DIGITAL VOLTAGE (ARDUINO UNO EXAMPLE BANDS)
⚙ Step 1: Measure the voltage.
⚙ Step 2: If it is from 0 V to 0.5 V, it is a digital 0.
⚙ Step 3: If it is from 3.5 V to 5 V, it is a digital 1.
⚙ Step 4: If it is between 0.5 V and 3.5 V, it is in the undefined region, and the result cannot be predicted.

EXAMPLE: CLASSIFY THREE VOLTAGES
Added example:
⚙ 0.3 V is between 0 and 0.5 V, so it is a digital 0.
⚙ 4.2 V is between 3.5 and 5 V, so it is a digital 1.
⚙ 2.0 V is between 0.5 and 3.5 V, so it is undefined. It might be read as 0 or 1.

COMPARING ANALOG DATA AND DIGITAL DATA
⚙ Nature: analog is continuous. Digital is discrete.
⚙ Range: analog is infinite. Digital is finite and limited.
⚙ Noise sensitivity: analog is high. Digital is low (less susceptible to noise).
⚙ Storage: analog is harder to store digitally. Digital is easy to store in binary form.
⚙ Error correction: analog is difficult. Digital is easier, because errors can be detected.
⚙ Examples: analog includes sound waves and temperature. Digital includes binary code and digital audio.
Difference: "continuous" describes how analog changes (smoothly). "Infinite range" describes how many values analog can take (any value).
Difference: "discrete" describes how digital changes (in steps). "Finite set of values" describes how many values digital can take (a limited number).

ANALOG VS DIGITAL VISUALIZATION
A common comparison is a smooth audio waveform (analog) versus a stepped digital representation of it (a sampling of that waveform).
★ In digital data, each sampled point stands for a fixed interval of the original waveform, which creates a "stepped" approximation of the continuous analog wave.
(a) Analog waveform: a smooth, straight ramp that rises continuously.
(b) Digitized waveform: the same ramp drawn as a staircase of small steps.
EASIER: The more steps you use (and the smaller each step), the closer the staircase looks to the smooth ramp.

HOW TO DIGITIZE AN ANALOG SIGNAL
Added example: these are the standard steps behind the stepped waveform and PCM.
★ Sampling: measuring the analog signal at regular time intervals.
★ Sample rate: how many samples are taken per second, measured in hertz (Hz).
★ Quantization: rounding each sample to the nearest allowed level.
★ With n bits per sample, there are 2^n levels.
⚙ Step 1: Sample the signal at regular time intervals.
⚙ Step 2: Quantize each sample by rounding it to the nearest allowed level.
⚙ Step 3: Encode each level as a binary number.
Difference: sampling chops the signal in time (when we measure). Quantization chops it in value (which level we round to).

EXAMPLE: DIGITIZE FIVE SAMPLES WITH 3 BITS
Added example: levels are 0 V to 7 V in steps of 1 V. With 3 bits there are 2^3 = 8 levels, numbered 0 to 7.
⚙ Samples taken every 1 ms: 0.4 V, 1.7 V, 2.6 V, 3.2 V, 4.8 V.
⚙ Round each to the nearest level: 0.4 to 0, 1.7 to 2, 2.6 to 3, 3.2 to 3, 4.8 to 5.
⚙ Encode in 3-bit binary: 0 = 000, 2 = 010, 3 = 011, 3 = 011, 5 = 101.
⚙ Answer: 000 010 011 011 101.
⚙ Note: 2.6 V and 3.2 V both became level 3. That small loss of detail is why the digital wave is only an approximation.

EXAMPLE: SAMPLE COUNT AND SAMPLE INTERVAL
Added example:
⚙ Number of samples = sample rate x time. At 8,000 Hz for 3 seconds: 8,000 x 3 = 24,000 samples.
⚙ Time between samples = 1 / sample rate. At 8,000 Hz: 1 / 8,000 = 0.000125 s = 0.125 ms.
⚙ Check: 24,000 samples x 0.000125 s = 3 s.

EXAMPLE: CD AUDIO BIT RATE
Added example: CD audio (PCM) uses 44,100 samples per second, 16 bits per sample, and 2 channels (stereo).
⚙ Step 1: Bits per second for one channel = 44,100 x 16 = 705,600.
⚙ Step 2: Both channels = 705,600 x 2 = 1,411,200 bits per second.
⚙ Step 3: In bytes = 1,411,200 / 8 = 176,400 bytes per second.
⚙ Check: 44,100 x 32 bits (16 x 2) = 1,411,200.

EXAMPLE: HOW FAST HOUSE AC CHANGES
Added example: the time for one full cycle (the period) is 1 / frequency.
⚙ At 60 Hz: 1 / 60 = 0.0167 s, about 16.7 ms per cycle.
⚙ At 50 Hz: 1 / 50 = 0.02 s = 20 ms per cycle.

COMMON MISTAKES
Thinking digital is perfectly immune to noise. It is less prone to noise, and a voltage in the undefined region can still be misread.
Thinking analog only means AC power. AC is just one example; sensors like accelerometers are analog too.
Calling 0 and 1 the only possible voltages. Digital uses bands of voltage, not two exact values.
Dividing time by the sample rate to count samples. Multiply instead: samples = rate x time.
Mixing up the table rows: analog has HIGH noise sensitivity and DIFFICULT error correction.

EXAM TIPS
Analog: continuous, infinite range, high noise sensitivity, hard to store digitally, hard to correct errors.
Digital: discrete, finite and limited, low noise sensitivity, easy to store in binary, errors can be detected.
Analog examples: voice, light intensity, temperature, analog clocks, vinyl records. Digital examples: binary code, digital images, digital audio (PCM on CDs), digital clocks, MP3 files.
For any example, ask: can it take any value in between (analog) or only fixed steps (digital)?

SELF-TEST
Q: Is a vinyl record analog or digital?
★ Analog. Sound is recorded as a continuous wave form.
Q: Which type of data has a finite set of values?
★ Digital data.
Q: What is the region between the low and high voltage bands called?
★ The undefined region.
Q: What must happen to an accelerometer's output before an Arduino can use it?
★ It must be converted from analog to digital.
Q: How many levels does a 4-bit digitizer have?
★ 2^4 = 16 levels.
