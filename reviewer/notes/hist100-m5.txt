Module 5 Agrarian Reform Policies
The history of agrarian reform, RA 6657 and CARP, and current updates (summarized)

THE BIG IDEA
Agrarian reform is not one law or a one-time handing out of land. It is a long historical process responding to unequal control of land.
⚙ Reading lens: historical problem, then policy response, then implementation, then consequences, then remaining issues.

KEY TERMS
★ Land reform: mainly changes who owns or controls the land (for example, transferring land from a landlord to a tenant).
★ Agrarian reform: broader than land transfer. It includes rights, support services, justice, productivity, and rural development.
★ Formula: Agrarian Reform = Land + Rights + Support + Justice + Productivity.
Difference: land reform asks "who owns the land?" Agrarian reform also asks "can the farmer stay secure and productive after getting it?"
★ Land tenure: the way land is owned, held, rented, or controlled.
★ Tenant: a farmer who works land owned by another person.
★ Landlord: an owner who controls land worked by tenants or laborers.
★ Hacienda: a large estate or plantation.
★ CLOA: Certificate of Land Ownership Award.
★ ARB: Agrarian Reform Beneficiary.
⚙ Historical analysis lenses: Continuity (what stayed the same), Change (what is new), Cause (why), Consequence (who gained or lost), Evidence (laws, dates, data).

HISTORY OF AGRARIAN REFORM, PERIOD BY PERIOD
⚙ Pre-colonial: land was tied to community use, kinship, and barangay customs, not privately titled property.
⚙ Spanish period: encomienda, haciendas, and friar lands; tribute and forced labor; many peasants became tenants. Land meant religious, political, and social power.
⚙ Revolution and First Republic: land grievances and anti-friar feelings fed the Revolution, but independence did not end unequal landownership.
⚙ American period: new land laws, registration, and bureaucracy. Legal reform still gave unequal results because poor farmers lacked money, documents, and literacy.
⚙ Commonwealth: tenant unrest, crop-sharing disputes, and debt bondage; the government used social justice language but elites stayed powerful.
⚙ Japanese occupation: war disrupted farming; the Hukbalahap rose in the anti-Japanese struggle.
⚙ Postwar Republic: the Huk Rebellion; land became a question of peace and stability, not just economics.

AMERICAN PERIOD LAWS
⚙ Friar Lands Act: the government bought and resold friar lands, but many tenants could not afford them.
⚙ Public Land Act: regulated public land; people with money and knowledge benefited more.
⚙ Torrens title system: formal land registration; customary users often lacked the needed documents.
★ Lesson: a legal reform can still produce unequal outcomes when access to money, documents, and literacy is unequal.

EARLY REPUBLIC LAWS (MAGSAYSAY ERA)
⚙ RA 1199, Agricultural Tenancy Act: regulated tenancy relations and recognized tenant rights.
⚙ RA 1160, NARRA: supported resettlement and land settlement, linking land access to peace-building.
⚙ RA 1400, Land Reform Act: created the Land Tenure Administration and expanded government purchase of estates.
These laws helped but did not end land inequality.

RA 3844 AND PD 27
★ RA 3844, Agricultural Land Reform Code of 1963 (Macapagal era): abolished share tenancy in favor of agricultural leasehold and treated tenancy as a structural problem.
★ PD 27 (1972, Marcos era): the Tenant Emancipation Decree. It transferred land to tenant-farmers on rice and corn lands only, through Operation Land Transfer, Certificates of Land Transfer, and Emancipation Patents.
⚙ PD 27's weakness: it covered only rice and corn, leaving out other crops and plantation workers.
EASIER: Share tenancy means the tenant gives a share of the harvest to the landlord. Leasehold means the tenant pays a fixed rent instead.

THE ROAD TO CARP
⚙ 1972: PD 27, rice and corn tenant emancipation.
⚙ 1987: the 1987 Constitution places agrarian reform under social justice after EDSA.
⚙ 1987: Proclamation No. 131 and EO 229 give a transition framework.
⚙ 1988: RA 6657 establishes CARP.

RA 6657 AND CARP
★ RA 6657: the Comprehensive Agrarian Reform Law of 1988, which created the Comprehensive Agrarian Reform Program (CARP).
★ Why "comprehensive": it covers public and private agricultural lands regardless of crop and tenancy arrangement, unlike PD 27.
⚙ CARP covers: Lands, Beneficiaries, Mechanisms (acquisition, valuation, compensation, distribution), Support services, and Justice (dispute resolution).
⚙ Beneficiaries include agricultural lessees and share tenants, regular farmworkers, seasonal farmworkers, other farmworkers, actual tillers of public lands, and qualified cooperatives. Beneficiary status is a legal matter, not based only on poverty.
★ Covered land: within CARP's legal scope. Exempted land: removed from coverage for a specific use or legal ground. Excluded land: outside CARP because of its classification.
⚙ CARP process Step 1: Identify the landholding.
⚙ Step 2: Acquire and value the land.
⚙ Step 3: Compensate the landowner.
⚙ Step 4: Award the land to qualified ARBs.
⚙ Step 5: Provide post-distribution support.
★ Landowner retention limit: 5 hectares. Qualified children may get additional area.
★ Just compensation: CARP is not confiscation; landowners are paid according to legal valuation and due process.
⚙ Beneficiary duties: farm the land productively, follow transfer and conversion rules, pay amortization (unless condoned), and join support programs.

GOVERNMENT INSTITUTIONS IN CARP
⚙ DAR (Department of Agrarian Reform): identifies land, screens beneficiaries, and distributes land.
⚙ Land Bank: land valuation and payment.
⚙ PARC (Presidential Agrarian Reform Council): policy coordination.
⚙ DAR adjudication bodies: resolve agrarian disputes.
⚙ Registry of Deeds and LRA: register titles and land records.

SUPPORT SERVICES AND DISPUTES
★ Land alone is not enough. Support services: credit, farm machinery, irrigation, training, organizations, and market access.
⚙ Common disputes: coverage, valuation, beneficiary qualification, installation (actually occupying the land), and conversion (changing land to non-farm use).
★ Ownership is the beginning of reform, not the whole reform.

CARP STRENGTHS AND CHALLENGES
⚙ Strengths: broader coverage than PD 27, more beneficiary categories, a social justice framework, institutions, and support services.
⚙ Challenges: land conversion, legal delays, elite resistance, support gaps, collective CLOAs, and continuing poverty risks.
★ Passing a reform law does not automatically change the social structure.
★ RA 9700, CARPER (2009): extended and amended CARP because distribution was still incomplete.

HACIENDA LUISITA
★ Hacienda Luisita: a large sugar estate in Tarlac and a major test case of CARP.
★ Stock Distribution Option: farmworkers received corporate shares instead of land parcels. Critics said shares are not the same as real control over land.
⚙ The Supreme Court's involvement shows that reform depends on courts, agencies, landholders, and beneficiaries, not just the law.

CURRENT UPDATES
★ Project SPLIT: splits collective CLOAs into individual titles or e-titles for clearer ownership. The DAR-World Bank project is extended up to 2027.
★ RA 11953, New Agrarian Emancipation Act: condones (cancels) qualified ARBs' unpaid amortizations, interest, penalties, and surcharges.
⚙ RA 11953 figures: P57.557 billion in debt, 610,054 ARBs, and 1.17 million hectares.
⚙ 2026 DAR targets: 80% of ARB organizations managing profitable businesses, and a 3.5% increase in palay yield.
Difference: earlier CARP focused on distributing land; today's focus is clearer titles, debt relief, productivity, and organizations.
