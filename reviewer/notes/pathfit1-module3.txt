3.0 Move for Life: Understanding Exercise and the MMFIT Framework
PATHFIT 1: Movement Competency Training

MODULE OVERVIEW AND LEARNING OUTCOMES
This module introduces the concept of exercise, its essential elements, and the importance of physical activity for holistic well-being.
EASIER: Holistic well-being means the health of the whole person: body, mind, emotions, and social life, not only a strong body.
The module also explores the MMFIT framework, an acronym that helps you structure and assess your fitness routine.
EASIER: An acronym is a word made from the first letters of other words. MMFIT is a memory tool: each letter stands for one part of your workout plan.
Through reflective tasks and collaborative activities (group work), students gain practical insight into designing and sustaining an active lifestyle.
The module has three topics: Topic 1 Exercise (with its four types), Topic 2 Elements of Exercise, and Topic 3 MMFIT Principle.
There are four intended learning outcomes.
Outcome 1: Define exercise and differentiate it from physical activity.
Outcome 2: Identify and explain the key elements of exercise.
Outcome 3: Evaluate and improve personal fitness plans by integrating the MMFIT framework.
Outcome 4: Appreciate the importance of regular exercise by reflecting on its benefits and integrating it into daily life.
EASIER: In short: know what exercise is, know how a workout is built, use MMFIT to plan and improve your own routine, and understand why moving regularly is worth it.

TOPIC 1: EXERCISE
★ Exercise is a planned, structured, and repetitive physical activity performed to improve or maintain one's physical fitness.
EASIER: Planned means you decide ahead what, when, and how long. Structured means it follows an organized pattern such as sets, routines, or a schedule. Repetitive means you do it again and again over days and weeks.
EASIER: Physical fitness is the body's ability to do daily tasks with energy and without getting too tired.
EASIER: Think of exercise as studying with a timetable and a goal, while ordinary movement is like glancing at a book only because you happened to walk past it.
Unlike everyday physical activities, such as walking to class or doing chores, exercise is intentional and often goal-oriented.
EASIER: Intentional means done on purpose. Goal-oriented means you are working toward a target, such as better stamina, stronger legs, or less stress.

EXERCISE VERSUS PHYSICAL ACTIVITY
★ While all exercise is physical activity, not all physical activity qualifies as exercise; the distinction lies in structure, purpose, and repetition.
EASIER: Physical activity is any body movement that uses energy. Exercise is the planned, goal-driven part of it. In the same way, all squares are rectangles, but not all rectangles are squares.
Added example: Walking from the gate to your classroom is physical activity but not exercise, because you did not plan it to improve your fitness.
Added example: A planned 30-minute brisk walk three times a week to build stamina is exercise, because it has a plan, a purpose, and repetition.
Added example: Sweeping the floor is physical activity. A scheduled Zumba class twice a week for fitness is exercise.

THE FOUR TYPES OF EXERCISE
★ The four types of exercise are aerobic, anaerobic, flexibility, and balance and coordination.
EASIER: Each type trains something different: aerobic trains endurance, anaerobic trains strength and power, flexibility trains how far your joints can move, and balance and coordination train control of the body.

TYPE 1: AEROBIC EXERCISE
★ Aerobic exercise is any physical activity that increases your heart rate and breathing for a sustained period, using large muscle groups rhythmically and continuously.
★ The term aerobic means with oxygen, highlighting how your body uses oxygen to meet energy demands during prolonged activity.
EASIER: Picture a long, steady jog. Your lungs and heart deliver oxygen to your muscles as fast as the muscles use it, like a steady fuel supply to an engine.
EASIER: Large muscle groups are the big muscles such as the legs, hips, and back. Rhythmically means in a steady, repeating pattern. A sustained period means a long time without stopping.
Key characteristics of aerobic exercise are listed next.
★ Aerobic exercise is continuous and rhythmic (steady, repetitive movement such as walking or cycling), of moderate to vigorous intensity (you can usually talk but not sing), and of extended duration (typically at least 10 minutes, often 30 to 60 minutes).
Aerobic exercise uses oxygen efficiently and improves cardiovascular and respiratory endurance.
EASIER: Cardiovascular means heart and blood vessels. Respiratory means lungs and breathing. Endurance is the ability to keep going without getting too tired.
EASIER: Talk test: if you can say a sentence but cannot sing a song while exercising, you are at moderate intensity, which is the usual aerobic zone.
Benefits of aerobic exercise are listed next, one per line.
Strengthens the heart and lungs.
Improves circulation (blood flow around the body) and lowers blood pressure.
Enhances stamina and energy levels.
Helps regulate weight and body composition.
EASIER: Body composition is how much of your body is fat compared with muscle, bone, and water.
Reduces stress, anxiety, and depression.
Boosts immune function (the body's defense against sickness) and sleep quality.
Examples of aerobic exercise: brisk walking, jogging or running, swimming, cycling, dancing (for example Zumba and aerobic routines), jump rope, and hiking.
EASIER: Brisk walking is walking faster than a stroll, with a noticeable rise in heart rate. Zumba is a dance workout set to lively music. Aerobic routines are choreographed exercise sequences done to music.

TYPE 2: ANAEROBIC EXERCISE
★ Anaerobic exercise is short bursts of intense physical activity where the body's demand for oxygen exceeds the oxygen supply available.
EASIER: The prefix an- means without, so anaerobic means without enough oxygen. In a 100-meter sprint you cannot take in oxygen fast enough to meet the demand, so the muscles use fuel they already store. You still breathe; the oxygen simply cannot keep up.
★ Instead of relying on oxygen, the body uses energy stored in the muscles, primarily through glycolysis, to fuel movement; this energy comes from stored glucose and phosphates.
EASIER: Glycolysis is the quick breakdown of glucose (sugar stored in the muscle) to release energy. The phosphates are the small store of phosphate-based energy compounds (ATP and creatine phosphate) already inside the muscle.
EASIER: Think of a power bank: the stored energy is ready instantly, but it runs out fast.
★ Anaerobic exercise is powerful, fast, high in intensity, explosive and brief, typically lasting less than two minutes, and it builds strength and power by targeting fast-twitch muscle fibers.
EASIER: Fast-twitch fibers contract quickly and strongly but tire fast. Slow-twitch fibers contract more slowly and resist tiredness, which suits aerobic exercise. Power means strength produced quickly.
Anaerobic exercise often includes rest intervals, because recovery is essential between efforts.
EASIER: A rest interval is a planned pause between hard efforts, like catching your breath between rounds of sprints.
Benefits of anaerobic exercise are listed next, one per line.
Increases muscle mass and bone density.
Boosts metabolism and fat-burning capacity.
EASIER: Metabolism is the rate at which your body uses energy.
Enhances speed, agility, and explosive power.
EASIER: Agility is the ability to change direction quickly and smoothly.
Improves performance in competitive sports.
Strengthens joints and reduces injury risk.
Examples of anaerobic exercise: sprinting (100 m, 200 m), high-intensity interval training (HIIT), weightlifting, plyometrics (for example jump squats and box jumps), push-ups and pull-ups when done in short, intense sets, and sports drills (for example basketball suicides and football tackles).
EASIER: HIIT alternates very hard short efforts with short rests. Plyometrics are explosive jumping exercises. Basketball suicides are back-and-forth sprints to lines placed at increasing distances on the court.

AEROBIC VERSUS ANAEROBIC
Aerobic: oxygen supply meets oxygen demand, so the effort is steady and continuous.
Aerobic: moderate to vigorous intensity, at least 10 minutes and often 30 to 60 minutes, and it mainly builds endurance (examples: jogging, swimming).
Anaerobic: oxygen demand exceeds supply, so the effort is explosive and brief.
Anaerobic: high intensity, typically less than two minutes, with rest intervals, and it builds strength and power (examples: sprinting, weightlifting).
EASIER: Aerobic is like a marathon (long and steady). Anaerobic is like a sprint (short and all-out).

TYPE 3: FLEXIBILITY EXERCISE
★ Flexibility exercise is physical activity designed to improve the range of motion of muscles and joints.
EASIER: Range of motion is how far a joint can move in a healthy way, for example how high you can lift your arm. Flexibility work is like loosening a stiff door hinge so it swings farther.
These exercises help lengthen muscles, reduce stiffness, and enhance mobility, making everyday movements smoother and safer.
EASIER: Mobility means ease of movement.
Key characteristics of flexibility exercise are listed next.
It targets joints and muscles, especially those prone to tightness (for example the hamstrings, shoulders, and hips).
EASIER: The hamstrings are the muscles at the back of your thigh.
It involves stretching, which can be static (held) or dynamic (moving).
It improves posture and alignment and reduces muscular imbalances.
EASIER: A muscular imbalance is when one muscle group is much tighter or stronger than its opposite group.
It supports recovery and is often used during warm-up or cool-down phases.
Benefits of flexibility exercise are listed next, one per line.
Increases joint mobility and muscle elasticity.
Reduces risk of injury and muscle strain.
Enhances athletic performance and coordination.
Improves posture and reduces back pain.
Supports relaxation and stress relief.
Types of flexibility exercise are listed next.
★ Static stretching means holding a stretch for 15 to 60 seconds; example: seated hamstring stretch.
★ Dynamic stretching means controlled movements through the full range of motion; examples: arm circles and leg swings.
★ Proprioceptive Neuromuscular Facilitation (PNF) stretching is a contract-relax technique done with a partner; example: assisted hamstring stretch.
★ Ballistic stretching uses bouncing movements and is less recommended; example: quick toe touches.
EASIER: Proprioceptive means about the body's sense of where its parts are. Neuromuscular means nerves and muscles together. Facilitation means making something easier. PNF uses a muscle contraction to help the muscle relax and stretch farther.
EASIER: Ballistic stretching is less recommended because bouncing can push a muscle too far, too fast, and may strain it.

STATIC VERSUS DYNAMIC STRETCHING
Static stretching is held still. Dynamic stretching keeps moving.
EASIER: Memory trick: Static stays still; Dynamic does movement.
Dynamic stretching is done before activity (warm-up). Static stretching is done after activity (cool-down).
Added example: Dynamic: 10 leg swings on each leg before running. Static: hold a seated hamstring stretch for 30 seconds after running.
Added example: One contract-relax PNF repetition for the hamstrings follows these steps.
⚙ Step 1: Lie on your back. A partner gently lifts your straight leg until you feel a mild stretch.
⚙ Step 2: Push your leg against the partner's hand for a few seconds (the contract part). The partner keeps the leg still.
⚙ Step 3: Relax the leg completely (the relax part).
⚙ Step 4: The partner moves the leg slightly further and you hold the new stretch.

TYPE 4: BALANCE AND COORDINATION EXERCISE
Balance and coordination exercises are physical activities designed to improve your ability to control body movements, maintain stability, and synchronize muscle actions.
EASIER: Stability means staying steady. To synchronize means to make your muscles work together at the right moment.
These skills are vital not only for athletic performance but also for everyday activities such as walking, climbing stairs, and preventing falls.
★ Balance is the ability of the body to maintain control of its position, whether stationary or moving, without falling; static balance is holding a position (for example standing on one leg) and dynamic balance is maintaining control while moving (for example walking on a beam).
★ Balance relies on inputs from the vestibular system (inner ear), proprioception (body awareness), and visual cues (sight).
EASIER: Your body balances with three sensors: the inner ear senses head tilt and movement, proprioception senses where your joints and limbs are, and your eyes watch the surroundings. Close your eyes while standing on one leg and it gets harder, because you removed one sensor.
★ Coordination is the ability to execute smooth, accurate, and controlled movements by using multiple body parts together; its kinds are hand-eye coordination (catching a ball, writing), foot-eye coordination (kicking, dribbling), and bilateral coordination (using both sides of the body simultaneously).
Coordination relies on neuromuscular control, timing and rhythm, and motor planning.
EASIER: Neuromuscular control is how well your nerves direct your muscles. Timing and rhythm is doing each part of a movement at the right moment. Motor planning is the brain working out the sequence of a movement before you do it.
EASIER: Balance is about not falling over. Coordination is about moving several body parts smoothly together. Some exercises train both.
Examples of balance and coordination exercises are listed next, with their focus and description.
Single-leg stand: focus is balance; stand on one foot for 30 seconds.
Heel-to-toe walk: focus is balance and coordination; walk in a straight line placing heel to toe.
Agility ladder drills: focus is coordination; step patterns to improve footwork and rhythm.
Ball toss with partner: focus is hand-eye coordination; catch and throw while standing on one leg.
Yoga poses (for example Tree Pose): focus is static balance; hold the position while engaging the core and legs.
Tai Chi: focus is dynamic balance; slow, flowing movements that challenge stability.
EASIER: Engaging the core means gently tightening your belly and trunk muscles to stay steady. Tai Chi is a slow, flowing Chinese exercise. An agility ladder is a flat ladder laid on the floor that you step through in patterns.
Benefits of balance and coordination exercises are listed next, one per line.
Prevents falls and injuries.
Enhances athletic performance.
Improves posture and core strength.
Boosts reaction time and movement efficiency.
Supports healthy aging and functional independence.
EASIER: Functional independence means being able to do daily tasks, such as dressing and climbing stairs, without help.
Added example: To do a single-leg stand, follow these steps.
⚙ Step 1: Stand tall next to a wall or chair for safety.
⚙ Step 2: Lift one foot slightly off the floor and keep your eyes on a fixed point.
⚙ Step 3: Hold for 30 seconds, then switch legs.
Added example: To do a heel-to-toe walk, follow these steps.
⚙ Step 1: Choose a straight line, such as tape on the floor.
⚙ Step 2: Place the heel of your front foot directly against the toes of your back foot.
⚙ Step 3: Repeat with each step, keeping your eyes forward and your arms out for balance.

TOPIC 2: ELEMENTS OF EXERCISE
★ The elements of exercise are the essential components that make up a safe, effective, and well-rounded workout session: warm-up, workout (main activity), cool-down, and stretching.
These elements ensure that physical activity is not only beneficial but also sustainable and injury-free.
EASIER: Think of a trip: you start the engine (warm-up), drive (workout), slow down before parking (cool-down), and stretch your legs when you arrive (stretching).
EASIER: Well-rounded means complete, with nothing important missing. Sustainable means you can keep doing it for a long time.

WARM-UP
★ Warm-up prepares the body for physical activity by gradually increasing heart rate, blood flow, and muscle temperature.
Benefits of a warm-up: reduces risk of injury, enhances joint mobility, and mentally prepares the individual.
Examples of a warm-up: light jogging, arm circles, and dynamic stretches.
Keep in mind: think of the warm-up as the ignition of your workout, so never skip it.
EASIER: A cold rubber band snaps easily, but a warm one stretches. Your muscles act the same way.

WORKOUT (MAIN ACTIVITY)
★ Workout (main activity) is the part focused on achieving specific fitness goals, such as endurance, strength, or flexibility.
Benefits of the workout: builds cardiovascular health, muscular strength, or skill proficiency.
EASIER: Skill proficiency means being good at a skill, such as dribbling a ball.
The workout can be aerobic, anaerobic, or sport-specific.
Examples of a workout: running, circuit training, dance routines, and sports drills.
EASIER: Circuit training means moving through several exercise stations one after another with little rest.
Keep in mind: align the workout with MMFIT (motivation, mode, frequency, intensity, and time) to personalize your workout plans.

COOL-DOWN
★ Cool-down helps the body transition from high activity to rest, gradually lowering heart rate and preventing blood pooling.
EASIER: Blood pooling means blood collecting in the legs after hard exercise stops suddenly, which can make you dizzy. Walking slowly keeps the leg muscles pumping blood back toward the heart.
Benefits of a cool-down: reduces muscle soreness, aids recovery, and promotes relaxation.
Examples of a cool-down: walking, deep breathing, and gentle stretches.
Keep in mind: use cool-downs to reflect on wellness and mental clarity.

STRETCHING
★ Stretching enhances flexibility and range of motion, either before the workout (dynamic) or after the workout (static).
Benefits of stretching: improves posture and alignment, prevents tightness and injury, and supports long-term mobility.
Examples: dynamic stretches are leg swings and torso twists (before activity); static stretches are the hamstring stretch and shoulder stretch (after activity).
Connection to MMFIT: stretching supports both intensity and recovery, which are key to sustainable routines.
EASIER: Dynamic stretching before exercise prepares your muscles so you can work at the intensity you planned. Static stretching afterward helps your muscles recover.

WARM-UP VERSUS COOL-DOWN
Warm-up comes before the workout and gradually raises heart rate, blood flow, and muscle temperature.
Cool-down comes after the workout and gradually lowers heart rate and prevents blood pooling.
Warm-up uses light jogging, arm circles, and dynamic stretches. Cool-down uses walking, deep breathing, and gentle stretches.
EASIER: Warm-up is the ignition and cool-down is the brakes. You need both for a safe trip.

WHY THE CORE ELEMENTS MATTER
Take charge of your own fitness by planning routines that match your goals and energy levels.
Build healthy habits for life, so you can stay strong, active, and injury-free.
Adjust exercises to fit your needs, whether you are a beginner, an athlete, or someone with unique movement challenges.

TOPIC 3: MMFIT PRINCIPLE
★ MMFIT is an acronym for Motivation, Mode, Frequency, Intensity, and Time.
EASIER: Each letter answers one question: Motivation asks why do I move? Mode asks what kind of exercise am I doing? Frequency asks how often do I exercise? Intensity asks how hard do I work? Time asks how long do I exercise per session?
EASIER: Older versions of this idea are called MFIT (Mode, Frequency, Intensity, Time) or FITT (Frequency, Intensity, Time, Type). MMFIT adds Motivation at the front. In this module Mode and Type mean the same thing.

MOTIVATION (M)
★ Motivation is the reason behind movement, the why that fuels consistent exercise and wellness behaviors.
Motivation helps sustain commitment and builds a sense of purpose.
Motivation could be personal goals (for example weight loss, strength, stress relief), social reasons (for example team sports, peer support), or health needs (for example managing diabetes, improving heart health).
Why motivation matters: it sustains long-term engagement.
Why motivation matters: it shapes goal-setting and self-regulation.
EASIER: Self-regulation means controlling your own actions, such as getting up to exercise even when you do not feel like it.
Why motivation matters: it improves mental health and resilience.
EASIER: Resilience is the ability to bounce back from setbacks.
Why motivation matters: it supports autonomy and personal growth.
EASIER: Autonomy means making your own choices.
Remember: when you know your why, it is easier to stay consistent and overcome setbacks.
The four types of motivation come from Self-Determination Theory (Deci and Ryan) and PE motivation research.
★ Intrinsic motivation is driven by enjoyment, curiosity, or personal satisfaction. Examples: "I feel more focused after morning yoga." and "I enjoy dancing with friends, it lifts my mood."
★ Extrinsic motivation is influenced by external rewards or social expectations. Examples: "I want to look fit for graduation photos." and "I joined Zumba to earn PE credits."
★ Identified motivation is aligned with personal values or long-term goals. Examples: "I want to stay healthy so I can support my family." and "Exercise helps me manage academic stress."
★ Introjected motivation is based on guilt, pressure, or obligation. Examples: "I feel bad skipping gym when my classmates go." and "I exercise because I don't want to disappoint my PE teacher."
EASIER: Ask yourself what is pushing you. Intrinsic: the activity itself is fun. Extrinsic: a reward or expectation from outside. Identified: it matches what you value. Introjected: guilt or pressure inside your head.
EASIER: Intrinsic and identified motivation come more from your own choice. Extrinsic and introjected motivation come more from pressure or rewards. In the full theory, identified and introjected are forms of extrinsic motivation that differ in how far they have been internalized.

MODE (M)
★ Mode, also called type, is the specific category or form of physical activity chosen to achieve fitness goals; it answers the question what kind of exercise am I doing, determines the physiological systems being targeted (cardiovascular, muscular, flexibility, and so on), and sets the tone for the entire workout.
Mode is the foundational element of the MMFIT framework.
EASIER: Physiological systems means body systems, such as the heart and lungs, the muscles, and the joints.
★ The four modes in the recommended guidelines for college students are aerobic, resistance, flexibility, and balance/coordination.
Aerobic: sustained activity that increases heart rate; examples: running, swimming, cycling.
Resistance: builds muscular strength and endurance; examples: weightlifting, bodyweight exercises.
Flexibility: enhances joint range and muscle elasticity; examples: yoga, static stretching.
Balance/Coordination: improves stability and motor control; examples: Tai Chi, agility drills.
EASIER: Resistance training means making your muscles work against a load such as weights, your own body weight, or bands. It is mostly anaerobic. Yoga trains flexibility in stretching poses and static balance in poses like Tree Pose.
Tip: choose an activity you enjoy or want to improve in.

FREQUENCY (F)
★ Frequency is how often a person engages in exercise over a given period, typically measured in sessions per week; the general recommendation is 3 to 5 times weekly for moderate fitness, and athletes may train more.
EASIER: Frequency counts sessions, not minutes. Three sessions of 30 minutes is a frequency of three.
Recommended frequency guidelines for college students are listed next, based on WHO, ACSM, and the DepEd PE Curriculum.
Goal: general health. Frequency: 3 to 5 days per week. Mode: aerobic plus flexibility. Note: spread sessions across the week for consistency.
Goal: cardiovascular endurance. Frequency: 4 to 6 days per week. Mode: aerobic (for example jogging, cycling). Note: include at least 1 rest or active recovery day.
Goal: muscular strength. Frequency: 2 to 4 days per week (per muscle group). Mode: resistance training. Note: alternate muscle groups to allow recovery.
Goal: flexibility and mobility. Frequency: 3 to 7 days per week. Mode: stretching, yoga, dynamic drills. Note: can be done daily, especially after exercise.
Goal: weight management. Frequency: 5 to 6 days per week. Mode: aerobic plus resistance. Note: combine cardio and strength for best results.
Goal: stress relief and mental health. Frequency: 3 to 5 days per week. Mode: aerobic plus mind-body (for example yoga). Note: choose enjoyable activities to boost mood.
Goal: skill-based activities. Frequency: 2 to 3 days per week. Mode: balance, coordination, agility. Note: ideal for athletes or those in movement-based sports.
EASIER: Per muscle group means each muscle group, such as legs or arms, is trained 2 to 4 days a week, not the whole body 2 to 4 times in total. A rest or active recovery day means a day off or a very easy activity such as a slow walk.
Balance is key: too little will not bring results, and too much may lead to burnout or injury.

INTENSITY (I)
★ Intensity is the level of effort required to perform an activity, ranging from light to vigorous, and it can be measured using heart rate zones, the Rate of Perceived Exertion (RPE), and the talk test.
Intensity should match your goals, whether fat burning, endurance, or strength building.
EASIER: Intensity is how hard you work. Frequency is how often. Time is how long.
★ Intensity levels: light is gentle effort that is easy to maintain (leisure walking, stretching; you can talk and breathe easily); moderate is noticeable effort with an increased heart rate (brisk walking, dancing, cycling; you can talk but not sing); vigorous is high effort with rapid breathing and heart rate (running, HIIT, competitive sports; you can only speak short phrases).
EASIER: The talk test needs no equipment. Light: you could chat easily. Moderate: you can speak but not sing. Vigorous: you can only get out a few words at a time.
★ RPE (Rate of Perceived Exertion) scale: maximum effort is 10, vigorous effort is 7 to 9, moderate effort is 4 to 6, and light effort is at the low end of the scale.
EASIER: RPE means rating how hard the exercise feels to you on a scale of 1 to 10. Added example: light effort is about 1 to 3, which fills the gap between 1 and 4.
Heart rate zones are measured as a percentage of your maximum heart rate (the fastest your heart can safely beat).
⚙ Maximum heart rate (Max HR) = 220 − age.
⚙ Moderate intensity zone = 50 to 70 percent of Max HR.
⚙ Vigorous intensity zone = 70 to 85 percent of Max HR.
EASIER: To find a zone, first find your Max HR, then multiply it by each percentage. Convert a percentage to a decimal by dividing by 100 (70 percent = 0.70). Max HR = 220 − age is an estimate, so real hearts vary a little.
Start light and build up gradually. Intensity should challenge you, not overwhelm you.

TIME (T)
★ Time is the length of time spent performing physical activity during a single session; the typical range is 20 to 60 minutes, including warm-up and cool-down.
Duration depends on your goals and fitness level.
EASIER: Time is minutes per session. Do not confuse it with frequency (sessions per week).
Recommended time guidelines for college students are listed next, based on WHO, CDC, and the DepEd PE Curriculum.
Moderate intensity: 30 to 60 minutes per session; examples: brisk walking, cycling, dancing.
Vigorous intensity: 20 to 45 minutes per session; examples: running, HIIT, competitive sports.
Flexibility and balance: 15 to 30 minutes per session, 2 to 3 times per week (or daily); examples: yoga, stretching, Tai Chi.
Strength training: 30 to 45 minutes per session, 2 to 4 times per week (per muscle group); examples: weightlifting, resistance circuits.
⚙ Weekly target for moderate intensity activity: 150 to 300 minutes.
⚙ Weekly target for vigorous intensity activity: 75 to 150 minutes.
EASIER: The weekly target is the total minutes of the whole week added together. Added example: one minute of vigorous activity counts about the same as two minutes of moderate activity.
Why time matters: it ensures a sufficient stimulus for adaptation (for example endurance and strength).
EASIER: Stimulus is the challenge you give the body. Adaptation is the body getting stronger in response.
Why time matters: it supports habit formation and time management.
Why time matters: it helps balance effort and recovery.
Track your time to stay accountable and make sure you are getting enough movement.

WHY USE THE MMFIT FRAMEWORK
Using the MMFIT framework helps you take control of your fitness journey by making your workouts more personal, effective, and goal-driven.
It gives you a clear way to plan and reflect on your routine so you can stay consistent and improve over time.
MMFIT helps you stay motivated by connecting your workouts to your personal goals.
MMFIT helps you build smart habits by choosing how often, how hard, and how long you exercise.
MMFIT helps you monitor your progress and adjust your routine as needed.
MMFIT helps you create routines that fit your lifestyle, whether you are a beginner or an athlete.
MMFIT turns your workout from just moving into a meaningful plan that works for you.

EXAMPLE: MAX HEART RATE AND TARGET ZONES FOR A 20-YEAR-OLD
⚙ Step 1: Max HR = 220 − 20 = 200 beats per minute (bpm).
⚙ Step 2: Moderate zone lower limit = 200 × 0.50 = 100 bpm.
⚙ Step 3: Moderate zone upper limit = 200 × 0.70 = 140 bpm.
⚙ Step 4: Vigorous zone lower limit = 200 × 0.70 = 140 bpm.
⚙ Step 5: Vigorous zone upper limit = 200 × 0.85 = 170 bpm.
⚙ Final answer: Max HR 200 bpm; moderate zone 100 to 140 bpm; vigorous zone 140 to 170 bpm.

EXAMPLE: TARGET ZONES FOR AN 18-YEAR-OLD
Added example: This one shows what to do when the answer is not a whole number.
⚙ Step 1: Max HR = 220 − 18 = 202 bpm.
⚙ Step 2: Moderate zone = 202 × 0.50 = 101 bpm to 202 × 0.70 = 141.4, which rounds to 141 bpm.
⚙ Step 3: Vigorous zone = 202 × 0.70 = 141.4 (about 141 bpm) to 202 × 0.85 = 171.7, which rounds to 172 bpm.
⚙ Final answer: Moderate zone 101 to 141 bpm; vigorous zone 141 to 172 bpm.

EXAMPLE: CHECKING YOUR HEART RATE DURING EXERCISE
Added example: A 20-year-old counts the pulse at the wrist for 10 seconds right after jogging and gets 24 beats.
⚙ Step 1: Multiply by 6 to get beats per minute: 24 × 6 = 144 bpm.
⚙ Step 2: Compare with the zones for age 20 (moderate 100 to 140 bpm, vigorous 140 to 170 bpm).
⚙ Final answer: 144 bpm is just inside the vigorous zone.
Added example: Another 20-year-old counts 30 beats in 15 seconds. Multiply by 4: 30 × 4 = 120 bpm, which is in the moderate zone.

EXAMPLE: COUNTING WEEKLY MINUTES
Added example: Brisk walking for 30 minutes on 5 days.
⚙ Step 1: 30 × 5 = 150 minutes per week of moderate activity.
⚙ Final answer: 150 minutes meets the lower end of the 150 to 300 minute moderate target.
Added example: Running for 25 minutes on 3 days.
⚙ Step 1: 25 × 3 = 75 minutes per week of vigorous activity.
⚙ Final answer: 75 minutes meets the lower end of the 75 to 150 minute vigorous target, and 25 minutes per session is inside the 20 to 45 minute vigorous range.
Added example: A mixed week of 60 minutes moderate and 45 minutes vigorous.
⚙ Step 1: One vigorous minute counts as about two moderate minutes, so 45 × 2 = 90 moderate-equivalent minutes.
⚙ Step 2: 60 + 90 = 150 moderate-equivalent minutes.
⚙ Final answer: The week meets the minimum target.

EXAMPLE: BUILDING A PERSONAL MMFIT PLAN
Added example: Ana is 19 and wants to handle exam stress. She enjoys dancing.
⚙ Motivation: "Exercise helps me manage academic stress" (identified) plus "I enjoy dancing" (intrinsic).
⚙ Mode: aerobic (Zumba), plus yoga as a mind-body activity.
⚙ Frequency: stress relief and mental health is 3 to 5 days per week, so she chooses 5 days.
⚙ Intensity: moderate (talk but not sing, RPE 4 to 6). Max HR = 220 − 19 = 201; 201 × 0.50 = 100.5 and 201 × 0.70 = 140.7, so her zone is about 101 to 141 bpm.
⚙ Time: 45 minutes per session = 5 minutes warm-up + 35 minutes workout + 5 minutes cool-down, which is inside the 20 to 60 minute range.
⚙ Check: 35 minutes × 5 days = 175 moderate minutes per week, inside the 150 to 300 minute target.

EXAMPLE: A COMPLETE SESSION IN ORDER
Added example: A 45-minute jogging session for a college student.
⚙ Step 1 (warm-up, about 5 minutes): light jogging, arm circles, and dynamic stretches such as leg swings and torso twists.
⚙ Step 2 (workout, 30 minutes): steady aerobic jogging at moderate intensity.
⚙ Step 3 (cool-down, about 5 minutes): walk slowly and do deep breathing to bring the heart rate down gradually.
⚙ Step 4 (static stretching, about 5 minutes): hamstring stretch and shoulder stretch, each held 15 to 60 seconds.
⚙ Final answer: warm-up, workout, cool-down, static stretching; total 5 + 30 + 5 + 5 = 45 minutes.

EXAMPLE: CLASSIFYING EXERCISE TYPES
Added example: A steady 40-minute jog is aerobic, because it is continuous, rhythmic, and lasts more than 10 minutes.
Added example: Six all-out 10-second sprints with long rests between them are anaerobic, because they are brief, explosive, and include rest intervals.
Added example: Holding a seated hamstring stretch for 30 seconds is flexibility exercise, specifically static stretching.
Added example: Walking along a balance beam is balance exercise, specifically dynamic balance.
Added example: Catching a tossed ball is coordination exercise, specifically hand-eye coordination.

EXAMPLE: CLASSIFYING MOTIVATION STATEMENTS
Added example: "I play badminton because it is fun" is intrinsic, because the enjoyment itself is the reason.
Added example: "I jog to win a gift voucher in the fun run" is extrinsic, because the reward is external.
Added example: "I want to keep my blood pressure healthy because I value a long life" is identified, because it matches a personal value.
Added example: "I feel guilty when I miss practice, so I go" is introjected, because guilt is the driver.

COMMON MISTAKES
Do not say all physical activity is exercise. Exercise needs a plan, a purpose, and repetition.
Do not think anaerobic means you stop breathing. You still breathe; oxygen demand just exceeds supply.
Do not mix up static and dynamic stretching. Static is held and done after activity; dynamic is moving and done before activity.
Do not choose ballistic stretching as the recommended method. It uses bouncing and is less recommended.
Do not confuse the mode list with the types list. The four types are aerobic, anaerobic, flexibility, and balance and coordination; the four modes are aerobic, resistance, flexibility, and balance/coordination.
Do not confuse frequency (sessions per week), time (minutes per session), and intensity (how hard).
Do not confuse extrinsic motivation (outside reward or expectation) with introjected motivation (guilt or pressure).
Do not forget the first M. The framework is MMFIT, not MFIT.
Do not skip the warm-up or the cool-down.
When finding heart rate zones, do not forget to multiply Max HR by the percentages. Max HR alone is not the zone.
Do not call the weekly total the time per session.

EXAM TIPS
Memorize the MMFIT questions: why, what kind, how often, how hard, how long.
Memorize the numbers: aerobic at least 10 minutes (often 30 to 60); anaerobic less than two minutes; static hold 15 to 60 seconds; session 20 to 60 minutes; frequency 3 to 5 times weekly for moderate fitness.
Memorize the weekly targets: 150 to 300 minutes moderate or 75 to 150 minutes vigorous.
Memorize the heart rate rules: Max HR = 220 − age; moderate 50 to 70 percent; vigorous 70 to 85 percent.
Memorize RPE: moderate 4 to 6, vigorous 7 to 9, maximum 10.
Use the talk test: talk but not sing means moderate; only short phrases means vigorous.
Remember the order of a session: warm-up (dynamic stretches), workout, cool-down, static stretching.

SELF-TEST
Q: What is exercise?
★ A planned, structured, and repetitive physical activity performed to improve or maintain physical fitness.
Q: What are the four types of exercise?
★ Aerobic, anaerobic, flexibility, and balance and coordination.
Q: What does aerobic mean, and how long does aerobic exercise typically last?
★ Aerobic means with oxygen; it typically lasts at least 10 minutes, often 30 to 60 minutes.
Q: How long does anaerobic exercise typically last, and which muscle fibers does it target?
★ Less than two minutes; fast-twitch muscle fibers.
Q: Which stretching type is held for 15 to 60 seconds, and which uses a partner and the contract-relax technique?
★ Static stretching is held for 15 to 60 seconds; PNF stretching uses a partner and contract-relax.
Q: What three inputs does balance rely on?
★ The vestibular system (inner ear), proprioception (body awareness), and visual cues (sight).
Q: What are the four core elements of exercise?
★ Warm-up, workout (main activity), cool-down, and stretching.
Q: What does MMFIT stand for?
★ Motivation, Mode, Frequency, Intensity, and Time.
Q: Name the four types of motivation and the one based on guilt or pressure.
★ Intrinsic, extrinsic, identified, and introjected; introjected is based on guilt, pressure, or obligation.
Q: What is the formula for maximum heart rate, and what are the moderate and vigorous zones?
★ Max HR = 220 − age; moderate is 50 to 70 percent of Max HR; vigorous is 70 to 85 percent of Max HR.
Q: What is the typical session length, and what are the weekly targets?
★ 20 to 60 minutes including warm-up and cool-down; 150 to 300 minutes moderate or 75 to 150 minutes vigorous per week.
